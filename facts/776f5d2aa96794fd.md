## Statement

Let \(A_0,A_1,A_2\subset\mathbb R^2\) be arbitrary compact
two-dimensional convex bodies satisfying
\[
\frac{A_0+A_2}{2}\subseteq A_1.
\tag{1}
\]
Put
\[
S=\bigcup_{j=0}^2(\{j\}\times A_j),\qquad
M=\max\{|A_0|,|A_2|\},\qquad
m=\min\{|A_0|,|A_2|\},
\]
\[
k=|A_1|,\qquad T=M+m+k.
\]
Then
\[
k\geq M+\frac m2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\max_{y\in S}h_S(y)\geq\frac{2T}{9}.
\tag{2}
\]

## Proof

We first prove the result when the three bodies are convex polytopes.  The
final part passes to arbitrary convex bodies without losing the nonstrict
area hypothesis or the nonstrict depth conclusion.

Compatibility implies that
\[
\operatorname{conv}\left(
(\{0\}\times A_0)\cup(\{1\}\times A_1)\cup(\{2\}\times A_2)
\right)
\]
is a compact polytope whose integer-height sections are exactly the three
given bodies.  Thus the accepted certificate facts for the locked problem
apply.

Let
\[
\mu=\sum_{j=0}^2
\mathcal H^2\!\restriction(\{j\}\times A_j).
\]
Assume, for contradiction, that
\[
h_S(y)<t:=\frac{2T}{9}\qquad(y\in S).
\tag{3}
\]
Accepted fact `d2d117bcc58a1ba0` gives a strict finite-slope normalized
three-cap certificate.  Accepted fact `fdfc3e7459f0e194` audits its
orientation.  After reflecting the height coordinate if necessary, there
are proper closed halfspaces
\[
H_i=\{(h,w):f_i(w)+s_i(h-1)\leq0\},\qquad i=1,2,3,
\tag{4}
\]
such that
\[
\mu(H_i)<t,\qquad f_1+f_2+f_3=-1,\qquad
\Sigma:=s_1+s_2+s_3<-1,
\tag{5}
\]
and in this orientation
\[
|A_0|=M>|A_2|=m.
\tag{6}
\]
At height \(1\), the sum of the three defining functions is \(-1\).
At height \(2\), it is \(-1+\Sigma<0\).  Hence at every point of either
plane at least one defining function is strictly negative.  Thus the
interiors of the \(H_i\) cover the whole planes \(h=1\) and \(h=2\), not
merely \(A_1\) and \(A_2\).  This is the certificate orientation used
below.  No cover of height \(0\) is asserted.

Let
\[
\bar\mu=\frac1T\sum_{j=0}^2
\mathcal H^2\!\restriction(\{j\}\times A_j)
\tag{7}
\]
and let \(X=\operatorname{conv}S\).  For each \(i\), define on \(X\)
\[
G_{i,\varepsilon}(x)=
\max\left\{0,1-\frac{\operatorname{dist}(x,H_i)}{\varepsilon}\right\}.
\]
As \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\), these continuous functions decrease
pointwise to \(\mathbf 1_{H_i}\).  Dominated convergence and the strict
inequality in (5) therefore allow a choice of continuous functions \(G_i\)
such that
\[
G_i\geq\mathbf 1_{H_i},\qquad
\gamma_i:=\int G_i\,d\bar\mu<\frac29.
\tag{8}
\]
Set
\[
F_1=(G_1-\gamma_1)-(G_3-\gamma_3),\qquad
F_2=(G_2-\gamma_2)-(G_3-\gamma_3).
\tag{9}
\]
Both functions are continuous on \(X\) and have \(\bar\mu\)-mean zero.

We now construct the required two-balanced mixture.  Fix a dense sequence
\((x_r)_{r\geq1}\) in \(\mathbb R^3\) such that the height coordinate of
every \(x_r\) is nonintegral.  Start with the compact full-dimensional
convex cell \(K_\varnothing=X\).  Suppose \(K\) is a current
full-dimensional cell of positive \(\bar\mu\)-mass satisfying
\[
\int_KF_1\,d\bar\mu=\int_KF_2\,d\bar\mu=0.
\tag{10}
\]
Choose as anchor the first \(x_r\) lying in \(\operatorname{int}K\).
For \(n\in\mathbb S^2\), let \(Q_n^\pm\) be the two closed halfspaces
bounded by the plane through this anchor with normal \(n\), and put
\[
\Psi(n)=
\left(
\int_{K\cap Q_n^+}F_1\,d\bar\mu-
\int_{K\cap Q_n^-}F_1\,d\bar\mu,\
\int_{K\cap Q_n^+}F_2\,d\bar\mu-
\int_{K\cap Q_n^-}F_2\,d\bar\mu
\right).
\tag{11}
\]
Every plane through the anchor has \(\bar\mu\)-measure zero.  A
nonhorizontal plane meets each integer-height slice in a line, and a
horizontal plane through the anchor misses all three slices.  Dominated
convergence therefore makes \(\Psi\) continuous, and
\(\Psi(-n)=-\Psi(n)\).  Borsuk-Ulam gives \(n\) with \(\Psi(n)=0\).
Together with (10), this says that both children \(K\cap Q_n^\pm\)
satisfy (10).

Retain only children of positive \(\bar\mu\)-mass and recurse.  A
zero-mass child receives transition probability zero and is never
normalized.  At a retained node \(K\), choose a child \(K'\) with
probability
\[
\frac{\bar\mu(K')}{\bar\mu(K)}.
\tag{12}
\]
The common cutting boundary has zero mass, so the probabilities in (12)
sum to one.  This defines a random nested branch
\[
K_0\supseteq K_1\supseteq\cdots
\]
of positive-mass compact convex cells and random probability measures
\[
P_n=\frac{\bar\mu\!\restriction K_n}{\bar\mu(K_n)}.
\tag{13}
\]

For every \(\varphi\in C(X)\), the random variables
\[
Z_n^\varphi=\int\varphi\,dP_n
\]
form a bounded martingale: the child-mass average of the two normalized
restrictions is the normalized parent restriction.  Choose a countable
sup-norm dense subset of \(C(X)\).  Bounded martingale convergence,
simultaneously on this subset, and weak compactness of the probability
measures on \(X\) give an almost sure weak limit \(P\).  The dense test
functions make the limit unique, so the entire sequence \(P_n\), not just a
subsequence, converges weakly.  If \(\Pi\) is the law of \(P\), bounded
convergence gives, for every \(\varphi\in C(X)\),
\[
\int_X\varphi\,d\bar\mu
=
\int\left(\int_X\varphi\,dP\right)d\Pi(P).
\tag{14}
\]
This is the asserted barycentric identity.  Equation (10), divided by the
positive cell mass and passed to the weak limit, gives
\[
\int F_1\,dP=\int F_2\,dP=0
\qquad\text{for \(\Pi\)-almost every \(P\)}.
\tag{15}
\]

It remains to prove the carrier claim.  Put
\[
K_\infty=\bigcap_{n\geq0}K_n.
\]
The available indices in the fixed dense anchor sequence can only
disappear as the cells shrink.  Once an index is selected, its point lies
on the cutting boundary and is not interior to either child.  Consequently
the selected anchor indices along a branch are strictly increasing.  If
\(K_\infty\) had nonempty three-dimensional interior, some \(x_r\) would
belong to that interior and hence to the interior of every \(K_n\).  At
every stage the first eligible anchor index would then be at most \(r\),
contradicting strict increase along an infinite branch.  Thus
\[
\operatorname{affdim}K_\infty\leq2.
\tag{16}
\]
Every open neighborhood of \(K_\infty\) contains all sufficiently large
\(K_n\), by compactness and nestedness.  Hence the weak limit \(P\) is
supported on \(K_\infty\).  It is also supported on the closed set \(S\),
because every \(P_n\) is supported on \(S\).  This proves the terminal
carrier structure, including branches for which
\(\bar\mu(K_n)\downarrow0\).

Return to the functions \(G_i\).  Equations (9) and (15) show that for
almost every terminal \(P\) there is a real number \(\sigma=\sigma(P)\)
such that
\[
\int G_i\,dP=\gamma_i+\sigma,\qquad i=1,2,3.
\tag{17}
\]
The barycentric identity (14), applied to the \(G_i\), gives
\[
\mathbb E_\Pi\sigma=0.
\tag{18}
\]
Write
\[
u=P(h=0),\qquad c=P(h=2),\qquad
p=\frac MT,\qquad r=\frac mT.
\tag{19}
\]
Since \(P\) and \(\bar\mu\) are supported at heights \(0,1,2\), the slice
indicators in (19) agree there with continuous polynomials in \(h\), for
example
\[
\chi_0(h)=\frac{(h-1)(h-2)}2,\qquad
\chi_2(h)=\frac{h(h-1)}2.
\]
Applying (14) to these polynomials gives
\[
\mathbb E_\Pi u=p,\qquad \mathbb E_\Pi c=r.
\tag{20}
\]

The two covered planes give the first pointwise estimate on every terminal
measure.  With \(\Gamma=\gamma_1+\gamma_2+\gamma_3<2/3\),
\[
1-u
\leq\sum_{i=1}^3P(H_i)
\leq\sum_{i=1}^3\int G_i\,dP
=\Gamma+3\sigma,
\]
and therefore
\[
\sigma>\frac19-\frac u3.
\tag{21}
\]

We next prove the support-restricted estimate for a nonhorizontal carrier
\(K_\infty\).  Its intersections with the three height planes lie in three
parallel lines, with a line allowed to degenerate to a point.  Let
\(P_j=P\!\restriction\{h=j\}\), so the three masses are
\[
|P_0|=u,\qquad |P_1|=1-u-c,\qquad |P_2|=c.
\]
Put \(a=\min\{u,c\}\).  If \(a>0\), choose endpoint submeasures
\[
\widehat P_0=\frac auP_0,\qquad
\widehat P_2=\frac acP_2
\]
of common mass \(a\), and let
\[
\lambda=\frac1a\,\widehat P_0\otimes\widehat P_2.
\]
This is a coupling with marginals \(\widehat P_0,\widehat P_2\) and total
mass \(a\).  If \(a=0\), take the zero coupling.  The midpoint of every
coupled pair belongs to \(K_\infty\) by convexity and to \(A_1\) by (1).
Thus
\[
\nu=P_1+
\left((x_0,x_2)\mapsto\frac{x_0+x_2}{2}\right)_\#\lambda
\tag{22}
\]
is a measure on the height-\(1\) carrier line of mass
\[
|\nu|=1-u-c+a=1-\max\{u,c\}.
\tag{23}
\]
When this mass is positive, choose a one-dimensional median \(y_1\) of
\(\nu\).  It belongs to \(\{1\}\times A_1\).  For every closed halfspace
\(Q\) containing \(y_1\), the intersection of \(Q\) with the carrier line
at height \(1\) is a closed halfline containing the median, or the whole
line.  Moreover,
\[
\mathbf 1_Q\!\left(\frac{x_0+x_2}{2}\right)
\leq \mathbf 1_Q(x_0)+\mathbf 1_Q(x_2),
\tag{24}
\]
because a halfspace is convex.  Integrating (24) against \(\lambda\)
shows
\[
P(Q)\geq\nu(Q)\geq\frac{1-\max\{u,c\}}2.
\tag{25}
\]
Hence
\[
h_P(y_1)\geq\frac{1-\max\{u,c\}}2.
\tag{26}
\]

If \(c>0\), a one-dimensional median \(y_2\) of \(P_2\) belongs to
\(\{2\}\times A_2\).  The intersection of any halfspace containing
\(y_2\) with the height-\(2\) carrier line is again a closed halfline
containing the median, or the whole line.  Therefore
\[
h_P(y_2)\geq\frac c2.
\tag{27}
\]
Both candidates lie on planes covered by the \(H_i\).  If a covered point
\(y\) has \(P\)-depth at least \(d\), some \(H_i\) contains it, and (8),
(17) give
\[
\gamma_i+\sigma=\int G_i\,dP\geq P(H_i)\geq d,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\sigma>d-\frac29.
\tag{28}
\]
If the measure in (23) is zero and \(u=1,c=0\), estimate (21) supplies the
same trivial \(d=0\) conclusion.  If it is zero and \(c=1\), (27) applies.
Consequently every nonhorizontal terminal satisfies
\[
\sigma>
d(u,c)-\frac29,\qquad
d(u,c)=
\max\left\{
\frac{1-\max\{u,c\}}2,\frac c2
\right\}.
\tag{29}
\]
The estimate \(c/2\) in (27) and (29) has now been proved only in the
nonhorizontal case.

We separately audit every horizontal carrier.  If \(K_\infty\) is
horizontal, then its height is one of \(0,1,2\), because the probability
measure \(P\) is supported on the nonempty set \(S\cap K_\infty\).  Write
\[
B=\{w\in A_j:(j,w)\in K_\infty\}
\]
at that height.  If \(\operatorname{affdim}B=2\), then \(|B|>0\), and
continuity from above gives
\[
\bar\mu(K_n)\downarrow\bar\mu(K_\infty)=\frac{|B|}{T}>0.
\]
For every continuous \(\varphi\), the numerator
\(\int_{K_n}\varphi\,d\bar\mu\) decreases in indicator to
\(\int_{K_\infty}\varphi\,d\bar\mu\).  Hence
\[
P=\frac{\mathcal H^2\!\restriction(\{j\}\times B)}{|B|}.
\tag{30}
\]
Thus a two-dimensional horizontal terminal is normalized planar area on a
convex body.  Accepted fact `2f9be517307232c7` records the planar Grünbaum
bound, so the centroid of \(B\) has \(P\)-depth at least \(4/9\).  If
\(\operatorname{affdim}B\leq1\), any probability measure supported on
\(B\) has a median of depth at least \(1/2\).  This also covers point
supports and all zero-area limiting branches.

There are now exactly three horizontal cases.

1. If \(j=0\), then \((u,c)=(1,0)\), and (21) gives
   \[
   \sigma>-\frac29.
   \tag{31}
   \]
2. If \(j=1\), then \((u,c)=(0,0)\).  The carrier has a point of depth at
   least \(4/9\), and the height-\(1\) cover and (28) give
   \[
   \sigma>\frac29.
   \tag{32}
   \]
3. If \(j=2\), then \((u,c)=(0,1)\).  Again the carrier has a point of
   depth at least \(4/9\), so
   \[
   \sigma>\frac29.
   \tag{33}
   \]

In particular, (33), not a false universal \(1/2\)-depth assertion, handles
the two-dimensional covered endpoint.

Define the affine leaf ledger
\[
L(u,c)=\frac29-\frac{4u}{9}-\frac c3.
\tag{34}
\]
We claim
\[
\sigma>L(u,c)
\tag{35}
\]
for every terminal measure.  First suppose the carrier is nonhorizontal.
If \(u+3c\geq1\), then (21) proves (35), because
\[
\left(\frac19-\frac u3\right)-L(u,c)
=\frac{u+3c-1}{9}\geq0.
\tag{36}
\]
Suppose instead that \(u+3c\leq1\).  We need only check
\[
d(u,c)\geq\frac49-\frac{4u}{9}-\frac c3.
\tag{37}
\]
If \(u\geq c\), then \(u+c\leq1\) gives
\(d(u,c)=(1-u)/2\), and
\[
\frac{1-u}{2}-
\left(\frac49-\frac{4u}{9}-\frac c3\right)
=\frac{1-u+6c}{18}\geq0.
\tag{38}
\]
If \(c\geq u\), the condition \(u+3c\leq1\) gives \(c\leq1/3\), so
\(d(u,c)=(1-c)/2\), and
\[
\frac{1-c}{2}-
\left(\frac49-\frac{4u}{9}-\frac c3\right)
=\frac{1+8u-3c}{18}\geq0.
\tag{39}
\]
Equations (29) and (37) prove (35).

For horizontal carriers, the three cases above give the claim directly.
At height \(0\), (31) is exactly
\(\sigma>L(1,0)=-2/9\).  At height \(1\), (32) is exactly
\(\sigma>L(0,0)=2/9\).  At height \(2\), (33) is stronger than
\(\sigma>L(0,1)=-1/9\).  Thus (35) holds \(\Pi\)-almost surely in all
carrier dimensions and orientations.

Average (35) and use (18), (20).  Since the difference in (35) is a
strictly positive bounded random variable, its expectation is positive:
\[
0=\mathbb E_\Pi\sigma
>
\frac29-\frac{4p}{9}-\frac r3
=
\frac{2k-2M-m}{9T}.
\tag{40}
\]
Under \(k\geq M+m/2\), the last quantity is nonnegative, a contradiction.
This proves (2) for compatible convex polytopes, including equality in the
area hypothesis.

It remains to pass from polytopes to arbitrary compact two-dimensional
convex bodies.  Choose two-dimensional inscribed polytopes
\[
A_{0,n}\subseteq A_0,\qquad A_{2,n}\subseteq A_2
\]
converging to their bodies in Hausdorff distance, and circumscribed
polytopes
\[
A_1\subseteq A_{1,n}
\]
converging to \(A_1\).  They can be chosen monotone, so their areas and
their indicator measures converge.  Compatibility is preserved:
\[
\frac{A_{0,n}+A_{2,n}}2
\subseteq\frac{A_0+A_2}2
\subseteq A_1
\subseteq A_{1,n}.
\tag{41}
\]
The function
\[
(x,z)\longmapsto\max\{x,z\}+\frac12\min\{x,z\}
\]
is nondecreasing in each coordinate.  Therefore, with the evident
notation,
\[
k_n\geq k\geq M+\frac m2\geq M_n+\frac{m_n}{2}.
\tag{42}
\]
Moreover, the polytope
\[
C_n=\operatorname{conv}\left(
(\{0\}\times A_{0,n})\cup
(\{1\}\times A_{1,n})\cup
(\{2\}\times A_{2,n})
\right)
\tag{43}
\]
has exactly these three integer-height sections.  At height \(1\), any
additional convex combination from heights \(0\) and \(2\) lies in
\((A_{0,n}+A_{2,n})/2\subseteq A_{1,n}\), and there are no other integer
heights in \([0,2]\).

Apply the polytopal result to obtain \(y_n\in S_n\) with
\[
h_{S_n}(y_n)\geq\frac{2T_n}{9}.
\tag{44}
\]
After passing to a subsequence, the height of \(y_n\) is fixed and
\(y_n\to y\in S\).  The slice measures \(\mu_n\) converge to \(\mu\) in
total variation, and \(T_n\to T\).

Let \(H=\{x:a\cdot x\leq b\}\) be any proper closed halfspace containing
\(y\), with \(\lVert a\rVert=1\), and put
\[
\delta_n=\max\{0,a\cdot y_n-b\},\qquad
H_n=\{x:a\cdot x\leq b+\delta_n\}.
\]
Then \(y_n\in H_n\), \(\delta_n\to0\), and (44) gives
\[
\mu_n(H_n)\geq\frac{2T_n}{9}.
\tag{45}
\]
Total-variation convergence changes the left side by \(o(1)\), while
continuity from above for the family
\(\{x:a\cdot x\leq b+\varepsilon\}\), as
\(\varepsilon\downarrow0\), gives \(\mu(H_n)\to\mu(H)\).  Indeed, for
each fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), eventually
\[
H\subseteq H_n\subseteq\{x:a\cdot x\leq b+\varepsilon\}.
\]
Passing to the limit in (45) yields
\[
\mu(H)\geq\frac{2T}{9}.
\]
This holds for every closed halfspace containing \(y\), so
\(h_S(y)\geq2T/9\).  The approximation therefore preserves the required
nonstrict conclusion and completes the arbitrary-body proof.

## External sources

No external geometric claim is added beyond the planar Grünbaum inequality
already recorded in accepted fact `2f9be517307232c7`.  The construction
uses the standard Borsuk-Ulam statement that every continuous odd map
\(\mathbb S^2\to\mathbb R^2\) has a zero, and the standard bounded
martingale convergence theorem for real-valued martingales.
