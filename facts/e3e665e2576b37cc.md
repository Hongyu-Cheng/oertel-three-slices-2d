## Statement

Let
\[
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\rho,\sigma,\tau>0,\qquad
\alpha+\beta+\gamma=1,
\]
and assume
\[
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\gamma}\le \rho\le1+\frac{\gamma}{\beta},
\quad
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\le \sigma\le1+\frac{\beta}{\gamma},
\quad
\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\le \tau\le1+\frac{\alpha}{\gamma},
\]
\[
\sigma+\tau\ge1,\qquad
\frac1\sigma+\frac1\rho\ge1,\qquad
\rho+\frac1\tau\ge1.
\]
Define
\[
A_1=(\beta+\gamma)^2-\beta^2\rho-\gamma^2\sigma,
\]
\[
A_2=(\gamma+\alpha)^2-\gamma^2\tau-\frac{\alpha^2}{\rho},
\qquad
A_3=(\alpha+\beta)^2-\frac{\alpha^2}{\sigma}-\frac{\beta^2}{\tau},
\]
and
\[
b=\gamma^2(\sigma+\tau-1)
+\alpha^2\left(\frac1\sigma+\frac1\rho-1\right)
+\beta^2\left(\rho+\frac1\tau-1\right).
\]

If
\[
\sqrt{A_1}\ge\sqrt{A_2}+\sqrt{A_3},
\]
then
\[
b>2\sqrt{A_2A_3}.
\tag{1}
\]
The two cyclic analogues also hold. Consequently, in every unbalanced branch and for every \(0\le d_i\le A_i\),
\[
b>
\sqrt{d_1d_2}+\sqrt{d_2d_3}+\sqrt{d_3d_1}
-\frac12(d_1+d_2+d_3).
\tag{2}
\]

## Proof

Define the six endpoint slacks
\[
R_1=\beta+\gamma-\beta\rho,\qquad
R_2=\alpha+\gamma-\frac{\alpha}{\rho},
\]
\[
S_1=\beta+\gamma-\gamma\sigma,\qquad
S_2=\alpha+\beta-\frac{\alpha}{\sigma},
\]
\[
T_1=\alpha+\gamma-\gamma\tau,\qquad
T_2=\alpha+\beta-\frac{\beta}{\tau}.
\]
Every one is nonnegative by the corresponding endpoint bound. Direct expansion gives
\[
A_1=\beta R_1+\gamma S_1,\qquad
A_2=\gamma T_1+\alpha R_2,\qquad
A_3=\alpha S_2+\beta T_2.
\tag{3}
\]
Thus \(A_i\ge0\).

The side-order slacks are
\[
U_\gamma=1-S_1-T_1=\gamma(\sigma+\tau-1)\ge0,
\]
\[
U_\alpha=1-R_2-S_2
=\alpha\left(\frac1\sigma+\frac1\rho-1\right)\ge0,
\]
\[
U_\beta=1-T_2-R_1
=\beta\left(\rho+\frac1\tau-1\right)\ge0.
\]
Hence
\[
b=\gamma U_\gamma+\alpha U_\alpha+\beta U_\beta\ge0.
\tag{4}
\]
Equations (3)-(4) also give the exact identity
\[
A_1+A_2+A_3+b=1.
\tag{5}
\]

We first prove a scalar estimate.

LEMMA

Suppose
\[
0<a<1-\frac1{\sqrt2},\qquad
0<d\le\frac{(1-a)^2}{4},
\]
and
\[
0<L\le L_0:=(1-a)^2-\frac12.
\]
Put
\[
H=a+d,\qquad K=1+a^2-2d,\qquad J=1-a-2d.
\]
Then
\[
\frac{2aK}{H}-\frac{a^2KJ^2}{H^2L}
<a(2-a)+L.
\tag{6}
\]

Indeed, all denominators are positive. After multiplying the difference between the right and left sides of (6) by \(H^2L\), it remains to prove positivity of
\[
N(L)=H^2L^2+aHC\,L+a^2KJ^2,
\qquad
C=(2-a)H-2K.
\tag{7}
\]
Write
\[
w=(1-a)^2-4d\ge0.
\]
Direct substitution gives
\[
C=-\frac{a^3+4a^2+5a+2+(6-a)w}{4}<0
\tag{8}
\]
and
\[
2HL_0+aC
=\frac{(1+a)^2(1-6a+a^2-w)}4.
\tag{9}
\]
The vertex of the quadratic \(N\) is
\[
L_*=-\frac{aC}{2H}>0.
\]

First suppose
\[
w\le w_0:=1-6a+a^2.
\]
Then \(w_0\ge0\), so \(a\le3-2\sqrt2<1/5\). Another direct expansion gives
\[
4KJ^2-C^2
=-\frac{((1+a)^2-w)P_a(w)}{16},
\tag{10}
\]
where
\[
P_a(w)=8w^2+(44a-a^2-4)w
-7a^4+6a^3+29a^2+12a-4.
\]
This quadratic is convex in \(w\), and
\[
P_a(0)=-(1+a)^2(7a^2-20a+4)<0,
\]
\[
P_a(w_0)=-8a(5a^2-8a+2)<0.
\]
Therefore \(P_a(w)<0\) throughout \(0\le w\le w_0\). Since
\[
w<(1+a)^2,
\]
equation (10) yields \(4KJ^2-C^2>0\). Hence the discriminant of \(N(L)\) is negative, while its leading coefficient is positive. Thus \(N(L)>0\).

Now suppose \(w>w_0\). By (9), \(L_*>L_0\), so \(N\) is decreasing throughout \(0<L\le L_0\). It is enough to prove \(N(L_0)>0\). Put
\[
r=w-w_0>0.
\]
Exact expansion gives
\[
64N(L_0)
=8a^2r^3+C_2r^2-16a^3P_1r+64a^4P_2,
\tag{11}
\]
where
\[
C_2=16a^4-112a^3+18a^2+4a+1,
\]
\[
P_1=10a^2-30a+7,\qquad
P_2=5a^2-8a+2.
\]
Since \(a<1-1/\sqrt2<3/10\),
\[
C_2>1+4a-16a^2\ge\frac{19}{25}>0,
\qquad
P_2>\frac1{20}>0.
\]
If \(P_1\le0\), every term on the right side of (11) is nonnegative and the last is positive.

Suppose instead that \(P_1>0\). The polynomial \(P_1\) is strictly decreasing on \(0<a<3/10\), and
\[
P_1\left(\frac{32}{125}\right)=-\frac{77}{3125}<0.
\]
Hence \(a<32/125\). Define
\[
Q(a)=10a^6+44a^5+11a^4-36a^3+20a^2-1.
\]
Its derivative is
\[
Q'(a)=4a\left(15a^4+55a^3+11a^2-27a+10\right)>0
\]
on \(0<a\le32/125\), while
\[
Q\left(\frac{32}{125}\right)
=-\frac{148637294277}{762939453125}<0.
\]
Thus \(Q(a)<0\). The identity
\[
C_2P_2-a^2P_1^2=-2Q(a)>0
\tag{12}
\]
shows that the quadratic
\[
C_2r^2-16a^3P_1r+64a^4P_2
\]
has positive leading coefficient and negative discriminant. It is therefore positive for every real \(r\). Equation (11) again gives \(N(L_0)>0\). This proves the lemma.

We now return to \(A_1,A_2,A_3\). Suppose first that \(A_1\ge1/2\). Set
\[
P=\beta^2\rho,\qquad Q=\gamma^2\sigma,\qquad
L=P+Q,
\]
\[
X=\gamma^2\tau,\qquad d=\beta\gamma,
\]
\[
C_0=(\alpha+\gamma)^2-\frac{\alpha^2\beta^2}{P},
\qquad
E_0=(\alpha+\beta)^2-\frac{\alpha^2\gamma^2}{Q}.
\]
All denominators are positive. We have
\[
A_1=(\beta+\gamma)^2-L,
\tag{13}
\]
\[
A_2=C_0-X,\qquad
A_3=E_0-\frac{d^2}{X}.
\tag{14}
\]
Since \(A_1\ge1/2\),
\[
0<L\le L_0:=(1-\alpha)^2-\frac12.
\tag{15}
\]
Because \(L>0\), this also gives
\[
0<\alpha<1-\frac1{\sqrt2}.
\]
Moreover,
\[
0<d=\beta\gamma\le\frac{(\beta+\gamma)^2}{4}
=\frac{(1-\alpha)^2}{4}.
\tag{16}
\]

For any \(k>0\),
\[
(\sqrt{A_2}+\sqrt{A_3})^2
\le (1+k^2)A_2+(1+k^{-2})A_3.
\]
Using (14) and
\[
(1+k^2)X+(1+k^{-2})\frac{d^2}{X}
\ge2d(k+k^{-1}),
\]
we obtain
\[
(\sqrt{A_2}+\sqrt{A_3})^2
\le
(1+k^2)C_0+(1+k^{-2})E_0-2d(k+k^{-1}).
\tag{17}
\]

Choose
\[
k=\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha+\gamma}.
\]
Put
\[
H=\alpha+d,\qquad
K=1+\alpha^2-2d,\qquad
J=1-\alpha-2d.
\]
The identities
\[
(\alpha+\beta)(\alpha+\gamma)=H,
\]
\[
(\alpha+\beta)^2+(\alpha+\gamma)^2=K,
\]
\[
\beta(\alpha+\beta)+\gamma(\alpha+\gamma)=J
\]
give, by Cauchy-Schwarz,
\[
(1+k^2)\frac{\beta^2}{P}
+(1+k^{-2})\frac{\gamma^2}{Q}
\ge\frac{KJ^2}{H^2L}.
\tag{18}
\]
Substituting (18) into (17) yields
\[
(\sqrt{A_2}+\sqrt{A_3})^2
\le
\frac{2\alpha K}{H}
-\frac{\alpha^2KJ^2}{H^2L}.
\tag{19}
\]
The lemma, with \(a=\alpha\), now gives
\[
(\sqrt{A_2}+\sqrt{A_3})^2
<\alpha(2-\alpha)+L.
\]
By (13),
\[
\alpha(2-\alpha)+L=1-A_1.
\]
Therefore
\[
A_1+(\sqrt{A_2}+\sqrt{A_3})^2<1.
\tag{20}
\]
Using (5), equation (20) is exactly
\[
b>2\sqrt{A_2A_3}.
\tag{21}
\]

It remains to combine this with the dominance assumption when \(A_1<1/2\). Squaring
\[
\sqrt{A_1}\ge\sqrt{A_2}+\sqrt{A_3}
\]
gives
\[
A_1-A_2-A_3\ge2\sqrt{A_2A_3}.
\]
By (5),
\[
b-(A_1-A_2-A_3)=1-2A_1>0.
\]
Thus (21) also holds when \(A_1<1/2\). At \(A_1=1/2\), the preceding scalar argument is strict. This proves (1).

The cyclic relabeling
\[
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;\rho,\sigma,\tau)
\longmapsto
\left(\beta,\gamma,\alpha;
\tau,\frac1\rho,\frac1\sigma\right)
\tag{22}
\]
preserves all endpoint and side-order inequalities, leaves \(b\) unchanged, and sends
\[
(A_1,A_2,A_3)\longmapsto(A_2,A_3,A_1).
\]
Applying (22) once and twice proves the \(A_2\)-dominant and \(A_3\)-dominant cases.

Finally, let
\[
q(x_1,x_2,x_3)
=x_1x_2+x_2x_3+x_3x_1-\frac12(x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2).
\]
If \(\sqrt{A_1}\ge\sqrt{A_2}+\sqrt{A_3}\), then for fixed \(x_2,x_3\),
\[
q(x_1,x_2,x_3)
=-\frac12(x_1-x_2-x_3)^2+2x_2x_3.
\]
Since \(x_2+x_3\le\sqrt{A_1}\), its maximum over
\[
0\le x_i\le\sqrt{A_i}
\]
is
\[
2\sqrt{A_2A_3}.
\tag{23}
\]
Equations (1) and (23) prove the strict inequality (2).

If \(A_2,A_3>0\), the box maximum in (23) occurs at
\[
x_2=\sqrt{A_2},\qquad
x_3=\sqrt{A_3},\qquad
x_1=x_2+x_3.
\]
If \(A_2=0\), the maximum is \(0\) and occurs at \(x_2=0\), \(x_1=x_3\); the case \(A_3=0\) is symmetric. If \(A_2=A_3=0\), it occurs at \(x_1=x_2=x_3=0\). Nevertheless, none yields equality in the required inequality because (1) is strict.

Also, \(b\) itself cannot vanish. Indeed, (4) and positivity of the weights would force all three side-order slacks to vanish. Then
\[
\sigma+\tau=1,\qquad
\frac1\sigma+\frac1\rho=1.
\]
The first equality and \(\tau>0\) imply \(\sigma<1\), while the second is impossible because \(1/\sigma>1\). Thus the zero-\(A_i\) cases are strict as well.

Hence there are no equality cases in any unbalanced branch under the stated positive hypotheses. Boundary limits with a zero barycentric weight are outside the statement because the reciprocal endpoint parameters are then not defined.
