## Statement

Let \(A_0,A_1,A_2\subseteq\mathbb R^2\) be nonempty two-dimensional compact convex polytopes satisfying
\[
\frac{A_0+A_2}{2}\subseteq A_1.
\]
Put
\[
S=(\{0\}\times A_0)\cup(\{1\}\times A_1)\cup(\{2\}\times A_2),
\qquad
\mu=\mathcal H_2|_S,
\qquad
a_i=|A_i|,
\qquad
T=a_0+a_1+a_2.
\]
For \(p\in\mathbb R^3\), define
\[
\operatorname{depth}_{\mu}(p)
=\inf\{\mu(H):H\subseteq\mathbb R^3
\text{ is a closed halfspace and }p\in H\}.
\]
Thus \(\operatorname{depth}_{\mu}(y)=h_S(y)\) for \(y\in S\). For
\(i\in\{0,1,2\}\), \(u\in\mathbb S^1\), and \(c\in\mathbb R\), put
\[
G_i^u(c)=\left|A_i\cap\{w:\langle u,w\rangle\le c\}\right|,
\]
and for a planar convex body \(A\) and \(z\in A\), put
\[
d_A(z)=\inf\{|A\cap P|:P\subseteq\mathbb R^2
\text{ is a closed halfplane and }z\in P\}.
\]

If \(p=(\tau,z)\) has height
\(\tau\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)\), then its ordinary three-dimensional halfspace depth is exactly
\[
\operatorname{depth}_{\mu}(\tau,z)
=
\min\left\{
L(\tau),R(\tau),
\inf_{\substack{u\in\mathbb S^1\\ \lambda\in\mathbb R}}
\sum_{i=0}^2G_i^u\bigl(\langle u,z\rangle+\lambda(\tau-i)\bigr)
\right\},
\tag{1}
\]
where
\[
(L(\tau),R(\tau))=
\begin{cases}
(a_0,a_1+a_2),&0<\tau<1,\\
(a_0+a_1,a_2),&1<\tau<2.
\end{cases}
\tag{2}
\]
At the three supported heights, accepted fact `949ce2fdf2e1e240` gives the complementary exact profile:
\[
\operatorname{depth}_{\mu}(0,z)=d_{A_0}(z),\qquad
\operatorname{depth}_{\mu}(2,z)=d_{A_2}(z),
\tag{3}
\]
and
\[
\operatorname{depth}_{\mu}(1,z)
=
\inf_{u\in\mathbb S^1}
\left[
G_1^u(\langle u,z\rangle)
+
\inf_{\substack{c_0,c_2\in\mathbb R\\
c_0+c_2=2\langle u,z\rangle}}
\bigl(G_0^u(c_0)+G_2^u(c_2)\bigr)
\right].
\tag{4}
\]

For \(0<\alpha<T\), let
\[
K_\alpha=\{p\in\mathbb R^3:\operatorname{depth}_{\mu}(p)\ge\alpha\}.
\]
Then \(K_\alpha\) is closed and convex, and \(K_{T/4}\ne\varnothing\).
Consequently, for each admissible \(S\), the assertion
\[
\text{every }p\in K_{T/4}\text{ can be assigned some }
y\in S\text{ with }h_S(y)\ge\frac{2T}{9}
\tag{5}
\]
is equivalent to
\[
K_{2T/9}\cap S\ne\varnothing,
\tag{6}
\]
which is exactly the locked conclusion.

More explicitly, when \(K_{T/4}\cap S=\varnothing\), formulas (1)-(4)
identify the first missing implication with no geometric shorthand. From
some \((\tau,z)\), where \(\tau\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)\), satisfying
\[
L(\tau),R(\tau)\ge\frac T4,\qquad
\sum_{i=0}^2G_i^u\bigl(\langle u,z\rangle+\lambda(\tau-i)\bigr)
\ge\frac T4
\quad\text{for every }u\in\mathbb S^1,\ \lambda\in\mathbb R,
\tag{M1}
\]
one must infer
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\exists z_0\in A_0:\ d_{A_0}(z_0)\ge\frac{2T}{9},
\\[-1mm]
&\quad\text{or}\quad
\exists z_2\in A_2:\ d_{A_2}(z_2)\ge\frac{2T}{9},
\\[-1mm]
&\quad\text{or}\quad
\exists z_1\in A_1:\
\inf_{u\in\mathbb S^1}
\left[
G_1^u(\langle u,z_1\rangle)
+
\inf_{\substack{c_0,c_2\in\mathbb R\\
c_0+c_2=2\langle u,z_1\rangle}}
\bigl(G_0^u(c_0)+G_2^u(c_2)\bigr)
\right]
\ge\frac{2T}{9}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{M2}
\]
No implication from (M1) to (M2) is supplied by depth-region
nonemptiness or convexity alone.

Moreover, this equivalence cannot be implemented by proving
\(K_{T/4}\cap S\ne\varnothing\). Let
\[
\Delta=\operatorname{conv}\left\{
\left(\frac23,0\right),(0,1),\left(-\frac23,-1\right)
\right\},
\qquad
A_0=A_1=\Delta,\qquad A_2=\frac1{10}\Delta .
\tag{7}
\]
This is an exact compatible rational instance with \(T=201/100\). Its ordinary centerpoint region satisfies
\[
\varnothing\ne K_{T/4}\subset (0,1)\times\mathbb R^2,
\qquad
K_{T/4}\cap S=\varnothing.
\tag{8}
\]
Nevertheless the supported point \((1,0)\) satisfies
\[
h_S((1,0))
\ge \frac{101}{225}
=\frac{2T}{9}+\frac1{450}.
\tag{9}
\]
Thus (7) is not a threshold counterexample. It is an exact obstruction to any argument that rounds merely by intersecting the convex ordinary \(T/4\) depth region with a supported plane.

## Proof

First derive (1). Any closed halfspace containing \(p\) contains a parallel closed halfspace whose boundary passes through \(p\), obtained by translating the boundary toward \(p\). Hence the depth infimum may be taken over boundary-through-\(p\) halfspaces.

A nonhorizontal boundary-through-\((\tau,z)\) halfspace has, after normalizing its planar normal, the form
\[
\{(x,w):\langle u,w-z\rangle\le\lambda(\tau-x)\}
\tag{10}
\]
for some \(u\in\mathbb S^1\) and \(\lambda\in\mathbb R\); both choices
of side of every nonhorizontal boundary occur among these parameters.
Its intersection with the height-\(i\) slice is
\[
\{i\}\times
\left(A_i\cap
\{w:\langle u,w\rangle
\le \langle u,z\rangle+\lambda(\tau-i)\}\right),
\]
whose mass is the \(i\)-th summand in (1). The two horizontal
boundary-through-\(p\) halfspaces are \(\{x\le\tau\}\) and
\(\{x\ge\tau\}\). Their masses are exactly the two values in (2).
This proves (1). Equations (3) and (4) are exactly the endpoint and
middle-slice identities in accepted fact `949ce2fdf2e1e240`; the separate
horizontal terms at integer heights are already accounted for there.

We next give a self-contained depth-region and centerpoint argument. Fix
\(0<\alpha<T\). Then
\[
K_\alpha
=
\bigcap_{\substack{H\text{ a closed halfspace}\\
\mu(H)>T-\alpha}}H.
\tag{11}
\]
Indeed, if \(p\in K_\alpha\) and a closed halfspace \(H\) of mass
greater than \(T-\alpha\) omitted \(p\), then the closed halfspace
parallel to \(H\), on the opposite side and with boundary through \(p\),
would have mass at most \(T-\mu(H)<\alpha\), a contradiction.
Conversely, if a closed halfspace \(Q\) containing \(p\) had
\(\mu(Q)<\alpha\), then its open complementary halfspace would have mass
greater than \(T-\alpha\). It is the increasing union of closed
halfspaces lying strictly inside it, so continuity from below would give
one such closed halfspace of mass greater than \(T-\alpha\) that omits
\(p\), contradicting membership in the right side of (11).
Thus (11) holds for \(0<\alpha<T\), and it makes \(K_\alpha\) closed
and convex in precisely that range.

For completeness, apply (11) at \(\alpha=T/4\) without invoking an
unproved centerpoint result. Put \(B=\operatorname{conv}(S)\). Consider
the compact convex sets \(B\cap H\), where \(H\) ranges over closed
halfspaces with \(\mu(H)>3T/4\). Any at most four of them intersect:
otherwise their complements would cover \(B\), while the sum of the
measures of those at most four complements would be strictly less than
\(T\), contradicting \(\mu(B)=T\).

Here is the needed three-dimensional Helly step. Given five points
\(x_1,\ldots,x_5\in\mathbb R^3\), their five lifted vectors
\((x_i,1)\in\mathbb R^4\) are linearly dependent. Hence there are real
numbers \(\gamma_i\), not all zero, such that
\[
\sum_{i=1}^5\gamma_i(x_i,1)=0.
\]
The last coordinates force \(\sum_i\gamma_i=0\), while the first three
coordinates give \(\sum_i\gamma_i x_i=0\); this is an affine dependence.
Because the coefficients are not all zero and sum to zero, some are
positive and some are negative. Splitting by sign and normalizing gives
two disjoint groups whose convex hulls intersect, which is Radon's theorem
in \(\mathbb R^3\).

If a finite family of convex sets had empty intersection, choose an
inclusion-minimal empty subfamily and, for each member, a point in all the
other members. If the subfamily had at least five members, apply the
preceding Radon partition to five such points. The common Radon point
belongs to each of those five convex sets by using the convex combination
from the group not carrying that set's index, and it belongs to every
remaining convex set because all five points do. This contradicts
minimality. Hence every finite empty subfamily has at most four members.
The preceding four-set intersection property therefore gives the finite
intersection property, and compactness of \(B\) gives a point in every
\(B\cap H\). Equation (11) places that point in \(K_{T/4}\).

Now (5) and (6) are equivalent. If (6) holds, choose one point in
\(K_{2T/9}\cap S\); the same supported point can be assigned to every
\(p\in K_{T/4}\). Conversely, \(K_{T/4}\ne\varnothing\), so applying
(5) to any one of its points yields (6). Thus ordinary centerpoint
existence and convexity add no extra hypothesis beyond the locked target;
a rounding proof must use more than their mere existence. If the
centerpoint is already supported, (M2) is immediate because
\(T/4>2T/9\). Otherwise, (1) says exactly that its fractional profile
satisfies (M1), while (3)-(4) say exactly that the locked conclusion is
(M2). This proves the asserted identification of the first missing
implication.

It remains to verify the exact calibration. The triangle \(\Delta\) in
(7) has rational vertices, centroid \(0\), and area
\[
\frac12\left|
\det\begin{pmatrix}-2/3&1\\-4/3&-1\end{pmatrix}
\right|=1.
\]
Accepted fact `43e70f89f224c5d7`, with \(\varepsilon=1/10\), proves
compatibility and
\[
\sup_{y\in S}h_S(y)
\le \frac49+\frac1{100}
=\frac{409}{900}.
\tag{12}
\]
Since
\[
\frac T4=\frac{201}{400}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac{201}{400}-\frac{409}{900}
=\frac{173}{3600}>0,
\tag{13}
\]
no supported point belongs to \(K_{T/4}\). On the other hand,
\(K_{T/4}\ne\varnothing\) by the proof above. Every positive-depth point
lies in \(B\). Indeed, if \(p\notin B\) and \(b\in B\) is nearest to
\(p\), convexity gives
\(\langle p-b,q-b\rangle\le0\) for every \(q\in B\), whereas the closed
halfspace
\[
\{r:\langle p-b,r-b\rangle\ge\lVert p-b\rVert^2\}
\]
contains \(p\) and misses \(B\), so it has zero mass. Compatibility gives
\[
B\cap(\{i\}\times\mathbb R^2)=\{i\}\times A_i
\qquad(i=0,1,2).
\tag{14}
\]
Indeed, the height-\(0\) and height-\(2\) identities follow because those
heights are extreme. If \((1,z)\in B\), aggregate a convex representation
by height. The height equation makes the weights at heights \(0\) and
\(2\) equal, say to \(t\), and hence
\[
z=(1-2t)z_1+2t\frac{z_0+z_2}{2}\in A_1
\]
by the compatibility inclusion; the reverse containment is immediate.
Thus every point of \(B\) at integer height is supported and is excluded
by (12)-(13). A point of height strictly between \(1\) and \(2\) lies in
the horizontal halfspace \(\{x\ge\tau\}\), whose mass is only
\(a_2=1/100<T/4\). Heights outside \([0,2]\) have depth zero. Hence every
point of \(K_{T/4}\) has height strictly between \(0\) and \(1\), proving
(8).

Finally verify directly that this is not a threshold counterexample. For
\(u\in\mathbb S^1\), write
\[
F_u(c)=|\Delta\cap\{w:\langle u,w\rangle\le c\}|.
\]
The centroid calculation proved in accepted fact `43e70f89f224c5d7`
gives
\[
F_u(0)\ge\frac49
\quad\text{for every }u.
\tag{15}
\]
Since \(A_2=(1/10)\Delta\),
\[
G_2^u(c)=\frac1{100}F_u(10c).
\tag{16}
\]
For every \(c\in\mathbb R\), if \(c\ge0\), then
\(F_u(c)\ge4/9\); if \(c\le0\), then
\[
\frac1{100}F_u(-10c)\ge\frac1{100}\frac49.
\]
Therefore
\[
\inf_{c\in\mathbb R}
\left(F_u(c)+\frac1{100}F_u(-10c)\right)
\ge\frac1{225}.
\tag{17}
\]
Using (4) at \(z=0\), together with (15)-(17), gives
\[
h_S((1,0))
\ge\frac49+\frac1{225}
=\frac{101}{225}.
\]
Since
\[
\frac{2T}{9}=\frac{67}{150},
\qquad
\frac{101}{225}-\frac{67}{150}=\frac1{450},
\]
equation (9) follows. The ordinary \(T/4\) region can therefore be
entirely fractional even when the threshold conclusion holds with exact
positive slack.
