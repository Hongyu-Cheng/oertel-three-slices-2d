## Statement

Let
\[
\delta_0=10^{-30}.
\]
Let \(K,\Delta\subset\mathbb R^2\) be convex bodies, with \(\Delta\) a
triangle, and suppose
\[
|K\mathbin{\triangle}\Delta|\leq\delta_0|K|.
\tag{1}
\]
Suppose three proper oriented halfplanes \(H_i\) have open interiors
covering \(\mathbb R^2\), and
\[
|K\cap H_i|\leq\left(\frac49+\delta_0\right)|K|
\qquad(i=1,2,3).
\tag{2}
\]
Map \(\Delta\) affinely to the standard simplex and write
\[
H_i=\{\varphi_i\geq0\}.
\]
Normalize each \(\varphi_i\) by a positive scalar so that the Euclidean
norm of its vector of three vertex values is \(1\).  Then, after a row
permutation, those three vertex-value vectors are each within \(1/100\)
of the three permutations of
\[
\frac{(2,-1,-1)}{\sqrt6}.
\tag{3}
\]
The linear parts have unique positive Farkas weights.  When those weights
are normalized to sum to \(3\), each is within \(1/100\) of \(1\).

## Proof

All areas and symmetric-difference areas acquire the same determinant
factor under a nonsingular affine map.  We may therefore map \(\Delta\)
to
\[
\Sigma=\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb R_{\geq0}^3:
\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3=1\right\}
\]
and work in its affine plane.  We retain the names \(K,H_i\).

Put \(E=|K\mathbin{\triangle}\Sigma|\).  From (1),
\[
|\Sigma|\geq |K|-E\geq(1-\delta_0)|K|.
\]
For every \(i\), (1) and (2) give
\[
\frac{|\Sigma\cap H_i|}{|\Sigma|}
\leq
\frac{4/9+2\delta_0}{1-\delta_0}
\leq \frac49+3\delta_0.
\tag{4}
\]
The last inequality is equivalent to
\(22\leq27(1-\delta_0)\).  Set
\[
\varepsilon=3\delta_0,\qquad
\gamma=10^{-8},\qquad
\eta=10^{-3},\qquad
\tau=\frac{3\varepsilon}{2}.
\tag{5}
\]

We first prove the quantitative cap lemma used for each row.  Let
\(z=(z_1,z_2,z_3)\) be the vertex-value vector of an oriented affine
halfplane on \(\Sigma\), normalized by \(\|z\|_2=1\).  Let
\[
A(z)=\frac{|\{\lambda\in\Sigma:z\cdot\lambda\geq0\}|}{|\Sigma|},
\qquad
m(z)=\frac{z_1+z_2+z_3}{3}.
\]
If
\[
A(z)\leq\frac49+\varepsilon,
\tag{6}
\]
then
\[
m(z)\leq\tau,
\tag{7}
\]
and either
\[
\operatorname{dist}\left(
z,\left\{
\frac{(2,-1,-1)}{\sqrt6},
\frac{(-1,2,-1)}{\sqrt6},
\frac{(-1,-1,2)}{\sqrt6}
\right\}\right)<\eta
\tag{8}
\]
or
\[
m(z)\leq-\gamma.
\tag{9}
\]

To prove this lemma, put
\[
q=\frac49+\varepsilon,\qquad
\rho=\frac94-\frac1q.
\]
Direct calculation gives
\[
0<\rho=\frac{81\varepsilon}{4(4+9\varepsilon)}
\leq\frac{81\varepsilon}{16}<\gamma^2,
\qquad q<\frac12.
\tag{10}
\]

Suppose first that \(m(z)>0\).  A cap with at least two nonnegative
vertex values has area at least \(1/2\).  Indeed, if its values are
\(a,b,-c\), where \(a,b\geq0\), \(c>0\), and \(a+b>c\), its complement
has relative area
\[
\frac{c^2}{(a+c)(b+c)}
=\frac{c^2}{c^2+c(a+b)+ab}<\frac12.
\]
The case of three nonnegative values has cap area \(1\).  Thus (6) forces
exactly one positive vertex value.  After permuting the vertices, write
\[
z=a(1,-x,-y),\qquad a>0,\quad x,y\geq0.
\tag{11}
\]
Here \(s=x+y<1\), and the two edge intercepts give the exact cap formula
\[
A(z)=\frac1{(1+x)(1+y)}.
\tag{12}
\]
Put \(t=1-s\) and \(d=x-y\).  From (10)--(12),
\[
(1+x)(1+y)
=\frac94-\frac32t+\frac14t^2-\frac14d^2
\geq\frac94-\rho.
\]
Therefore
\[
6t-t^2+d^2\leq4\rho.
\]
Since \(0\leq t\leq1\), this implies \(t\leq4\rho/5\), and hence
\[
m(z)=\frac{at}{3}\leq\frac{4\rho}{15}
\leq\frac{27\varepsilon}{20}<\tau.
\]
If \(m(z)\leq0\), (7) is immediate.  This proves (7).

It remains to prove the dichotomy (8)--(9).  Assume
\[
m(z)>-\gamma.
\tag{13}
\]
The vector \(z\) cannot have no positive coordinate, because then
\[
m(z)\leq-\frac{\|z\|_1}{3}\leq-\frac13<-\gamma.
\]
It also cannot have at least two nonnegative coordinates.  If all three
are nonnegative, its cap has full area.  Otherwise write its values as
\(a,b,-c\), where \(a,b\geq0\) and \(c>0\), and set \(S=a+b\).
Condition (13) gives \(c<S+3\gamma\).  Since
\(a+b+c\geq\|z\|_2=1\), it follows that
\[
S>\frac{1-3\gamma}{2}.
\]
Consequently the complement fraction satisfies
\[
\frac{c^2}{(a+c)(b+c)}
\leq\frac{c}{c+S}
<\frac{S+3\gamma}{2S+3\gamma}
<\frac12+\frac{3\gamma}{2}.
\]
The cap fraction is therefore greater than
\(1/2-3\gamma/2\), which is strictly greater than
\(4/9+\varepsilon\) for the constants in (5).  This contradicts (6).

Thus (11) holds under (13), without yet knowing whether \(s\) is below
or above \(1\).  Normalization and (13) imply
\[
1=a^2(1+x^2+y^2)\leq a^2(1+s^2),
\qquad
a(s-1)<3\gamma.
\tag{14}
\]
If \(a\leq1/2\), then (14) would give
\[
1<a^2+(a+3\gamma)^2
\leq\frac14+\left(\frac12+3\gamma\right)^2<1,
\]
a contradiction.  Hence \(a>1/2\).  With
\[
u=s-1,\qquad d=x-y,
\]
we obtain
\[
u<6\gamma
\tag{15}
\]
and, using (10), (12),
\[
\frac94+\frac32u+\frac14u^2-\frac14d^2
\geq\frac94-\rho.
\tag{16}
\]
If \(u\leq0\), put \(t=-u\in[0,1]\).  Equation (16) gives
\[
4\rho\geq6t-t^2+d^2\geq5t,
\]
so \(t\leq4\rho/5\).  Together with (10) and (15), this yields
\[
|u|<6\gamma.
\tag{17}
\]
Rearranging (16) and using (10), (15), and (17), we get
\[
d^2\leq4\rho+6u+u^2
<36\gamma+40\gamma^2<37\gamma.
\tag{18}
\]
It follows that
\[
\max\left\{\left|x-\frac12\right|,
\left|y-\frac12\right|\right\}
\leq\frac{|u|+|d|}{2}
<3\gamma+\frac{\sqrt{37\gamma}}2.
\tag{19}
\]
For \(\gamma=10^{-8}\),
\[
\sqrt{37\gamma}<\frac{61}{100000},
\]
so the right side of (19) is less than
\[
\frac{30503}{100000000}.
\tag{20}
\]

Let \(v=(1,-x,-y)\) and \(v_0=(1,-1/2,-1/2)\).  The elementary
normalization estimate
\[
\left\|\frac v{\|v\|_2}-\frac{v_0}{\|v_0\|_2}\right\|_2
\leq\frac{2\|v-v_0\|_2}{\|v_0\|_2}
\tag{21}
\]
and (20) now give
\[
\left\|z-\frac{(2,-1,-1)}{\sqrt6}\right\|_2
<3\frac{30503}{100000000}
<\frac1{1000}.
\]
Here we used
\(\|v-v_0\|_2\leq\sqrt2\max\{|x-1/2|,|y-1/2|\}\),
\(\|v_0\|_2=\sqrt{3/2}\), and \(4/\sqrt3<3\).
Permuting the coordinates proves (8).  The cap lemma is complete.

We now apply it to the three normalized rows.  Let \(z_i\) be the
vertex-value vector of \(\varphi_i\), let
\[
m_i=\frac{z_{i1}+z_{i2}+z_{i3}}3,
\qquad
Pz_i=z_i-m_i(1,1,1),
\tag{22}
\]
and put
\[
w_1=\frac{(2,-1,-1)}{\sqrt6},\quad
w_2=\frac{(-1,2,-1)}{\sqrt6},\quad
w_3=\frac{(-1,-1,2)}{\sqrt6}.
\tag{23}
\]
The centered vertex vector \(Pz_i\) represents the linear part of
\(\varphi_i\) under a fixed linear isomorphism from the two-dimensional
space \(\{r_1+r_2+r_3=0\}\) to the dual of the affine plane of
\(\Sigma\).

We record why the Farkas weights exist, are positive, and are unique.
No two open halfplanes can cover the plane.  Otherwise their two caps
would cover \(\Sigma\), and (4) would give
\[
1\leq
\frac{|\Sigma\cap H_i|+|\Sigma\cap H_j|}{|\Sigma|}
\leq2\left(\frac49+\varepsilon\right)<1,
\]
a contradiction.

The three linear parts span a two-dimensional space.  If they were
collinear, every open halfplane would be a strict left or right ray in
one scalar coordinate.  A finite union of such rays covers the real
line only when the largest left threshold is strictly larger than the
smallest right threshold; the two rays attaining those thresholds
already cover the line.  This would give a two-halfplane cover, which
was just excluded.

Since the open interiors cover the plane, the system
\(\varphi_i(x)\leq0\) for all \(i\) is infeasible.  The strict Farkas
alternative therefore gives coefficients \(\alpha_i\geq0\), not all
zero, such that
\[
\sum_{i=1}^3\alpha_iPz_i=0,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^3\alpha_i m_i>0.
\tag{24}
\]
No coefficient can vanish.  If, for example, only \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\)
were nonzero, then
\(\alpha_1\varphi_1+\alpha_2\varphi_2\) would be a positive constant,
so \(H_1^\circ\cup H_2^\circ=\mathbb R^2\), again a contradiction.
Because the linear parts span dimension two, their dependence space is
one-dimensional.  Thus the positive weights are unique up to common
scale.  Normalize them by
\[
\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\alpha_3=3.
\tag{25}
\]

Call row \(i\) good if it satisfies (8), and bad otherwise.  The cap
lemma gives \(m_i\leq\tau\) for every row and \(m_i\leq-\gamma\) for a
bad row.  If
\[
A_B=\sum_{i\ {\rm bad}}\alpha_i,
\]
then (24) and (25) imply
\[
0<\sum_i\alpha_i m_i
\leq3\tau-\gamma A_B.
\]
Hence
\[
A_B<\beta:=\frac{3\tau}{\gamma}
=\frac{27}{2\cdot10^{22}}<\frac1{1000}.
\tag{26}
\]

There cannot be a bad row.  If there were, at most two rows would be
good, and their total weight would exceed \(3-\beta\).  For each good
row choose \(w_{j_i}\) within \(\eta\) of \(z_i\).  Since
\[
\langle w_j,w_k\rangle=-\frac12\qquad(j\ne k),
\tag{27}
\]
every positive combination of at most two vectors from
\(\{w_1,w_2,w_3\}\) has norm at least half the sum of its coefficients.
Using (24), the contraction property of \(P\), and
\(\|Pz_i\|_2\leq1\), we would therefore have
\[
\frac{3-\beta}{2}
<
\left\|\sum_{i\ {\rm good}}\alpha_iw_{j_i}\right\|_2
\leq3\eta+\beta,
\]
which is impossible by (5) and (26).  Thus all three rows are good.

Their three assigned vectors are distinct.  Otherwise they use at most
two vectors from (23).  Aggregating equal vectors and applying the same
two-vector estimate gives
\[
\left\|\sum_i\alpha_iw_{j_i}\right\|_2\geq\frac32,
\]
whereas (24) and goodness give the upper bound \(3\eta<3/2\).
After permuting the rows, we may therefore assume
\[
\|z_i-w_i\|_2<\eta=\frac1{1000}<\frac1{100}.
\tag{28}
\]
This proves the requested frame assignment.

Finally put \(b_i=\alpha_i-1\).  By (25),
\(\sum_i b_i=0\), while (23) gives \(\sum_iw_i=0\).  Equations
(24) and (28) imply
\[
\left\|\sum_i b_iw_i\right\|_2
=\left\|\sum_i\alpha_iw_i\right\|_2
\leq\sum_i\alpha_i\|w_i-Pz_i\|_2
<3\eta.
\tag{29}
\]
Using (27) and \(\sum_i b_i=0\),
\[
\left\|\sum_i b_iw_i\right\|_2^2
=\frac32\sum_i b_i^2.
\tag{30}
\]
Therefore
\[
|\alpha_i-1|=|b_i|
\leq\left(\sum_jb_j^2\right)^{1/2}
<\sqrt{\frac23}\,3\eta
=\frac{\sqrt6}{1000}<\frac1{100}.
\]
This is the required quantitative Farkas-weight estimate.
