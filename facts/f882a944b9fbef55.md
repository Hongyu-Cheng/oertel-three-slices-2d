## Statement

Let
\[
|A_{0,n}|=M_n=1,\qquad |A_{2,n}|=m_n=x_n,\qquad
|A_{1,n}|=y_n,
\]
after reflecting the height coordinate if necessary, and assume
\[
x_n\longrightarrow0,\qquad y_n\longrightarrow1.
\tag{1}
\]
Suppose every triple is a counterexample, and put
\[
t_n=\frac{2(1+x_n+y_n)}9.
\tag{2}
\]
There are affine automorphisms preserving the three height planes and
having one common planar linear part, and there is a subsequence, for which
the transformed bodies \(K_{i,n}\) satisfy
\[
B\subseteq K_{1,n}\subseteq2B,\qquad
K_{0,n}\subseteq4B,\qquad K_{2,n}\subseteq8B,
\tag{3}
\]
and
\[
K_{0,n}\longrightarrow T_0,\qquad
K_{1,n}\longrightarrow T_1,\qquad
K_{2,n}\longrightarrow L
\tag{4}
\]
in Hausdorff distance.  Here \(T_0,T_1\) are nondegenerate triangles
of the same positive area, while \(L\) is either one point or one
nondegenerate compact segment.  Moreover,
\[
\frac{T_0+L}{2}\subseteq T_1.
\tag{5}
\]

For each \(n\), choose arbitrarily a normalized minimal certificate from
accepted fact `d2d117bcc58a1ba0`.  Write its three middle closed caps as
\[
C_{j,n}=K_{1,n}\cap\{w:q_{j,n}\cdot w\le r_{j,n}\},
\qquad q_{j,n}\in\mathbb S^1.
\tag{6}
\]
Then, after permuting \(j=1,2,3\) for each \(n\), the three cutting lines
converge to
\[
\{\beta_j=1/3\},\qquad j=1,2,3,
\tag{7}
\]
where \(\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3\) are the barycentric coordinates of
\(T_1\).  Equivalently, the limiting closed caps are
\[
T_1\cap\{\beta_j\ge1/3\},
\tag{8}
\]
the centroidal vertex caps bounded by lines parallel to the three opposite
edges.  The conclusion is independent of the choices of the finite-stage
minimal certificates.

## Proof

First, convexity of the original three-dimensional polytope gives the
midpoint containment
\[
\frac{A_{0,n}+A_{2,n}}2\subseteq A_{1,n}.
\tag{9}
\]
Indeed, the midpoint of \((0,a_0)\) and \((2,a_2)\) belongs to the polytope
and lies in its height-one section.

We record two planar facts used below.  For a planar convex body \(K\), put
\[
\delta(K)=\max_{z\in K}\inf_{u\in\mathbb S^1}
\left|K\cap\{w:u\cdot(w-z)\le0\}\right|.
\tag{10}
\]
The planar Grünbaum inequality recorded in accepted fact
`2f9be517307232c7` gives
\[
\delta(K)\ge\frac49|K|.
\tag{11}
\]
Its sharp equality statement in dimension two is:
\[
\delta(K)=\frac49|K|
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
K\ \hbox{is a nondegenerate triangle}.
\tag{12}
\]
Here are the precise equality hypotheses.  If \(g\) is the centroid, then
the left side of (11) and Grünbaum give
\[
\frac49|K|\le d_K(g)\le\delta(K)=\frac49|K|.
\]
The cap-area function through \(g\) is continuous on \(\mathbb S^1\), so
some centroidal halfplane attains \(4|K|/9\).  After translating \(g\) to
the origin, the planar equality clause for Grünbaum says exactly that
\[
K=\operatorname{conv}(-z/2+D,z),
\tag{13}
\]
where \(D\) is a nondegenerate segment parallel to the cutting line and
\(z\) is a point on the selected side.  Thus \(K\) is a triangle, the
cutting line is parallel to the side \(-z/2+D\), and its selected side is
the centroidal cap pointing to \(z\).  This verifies both the rigidity in
(12) and the orientation of the equality cap.  For a triangle, the direct
barycentric calculation in accepted fact `43e70f89f224c5d7` gives the
same conclusion: its centroidal vertex cap has area \(4|K|/9\), while the
opposite side has area \(5|K|/9\).

We also need continuity, rather than a quantitative stability theorem.
If nondegenerate convex bodies \(E_n\) converge to \(E\) in Hausdorff
distance inside one fixed ball, then
\[
|E_n|\longrightarrow|E|,\qquad
\delta(E_n)\longrightarrow\delta(E).
\tag{14}
\]
To prove the second assertion, if
\[
z_n\longrightarrow z,\qquad u_n\longrightarrow u,
\]
then, away from \(\partial E\) and the line
\(\{u\cdot(w-z)=0\}\), membership in both the moving body and the moving
halfplane stabilizes.  Those exceptional sets have planar area zero.
Dominated convergence therefore gives convergence of the corresponding
cap areas.  Taking minimizing directions, then maximizing points, and
using compactness of \(\mathbb S^1\) and of the bodies proves both
semicontinuities for \(\delta\).  The same dominated-convergence argument
without the halfplane proves area convergence.  Thus (14) is exact even
though no modulus of stability is asserted.

We now construct the simultaneous gauge.  Let \(J_n\) be a common planar
affine map sending a John ellipse of \(A_{1,n}\) to the unit disk \(B\),
with its center sent to the origin.  Write \(d_n>0\) for the absolute
determinant of its linear part.  Planar John containment gives
\[
B\subseteq J_n(A_{1,n})\subseteq2B.
\tag{15}
\]
Choose any
\[
p_n\in J_n(A_{2,n})
\]
and apply the affine height shear followed by a common translation
\[
\Psi_n(h,w)=(h,w+(1-h)p_n).
\tag{16}
\]
It fixes the entire middle plane pointwise and sends the three planar
bodies to
\[
K_{0,n}=J_n(A_{0,n})+p_n,\qquad
K_{1,n}=J_n(A_{1,n}),\qquad
K_{2,n}=J_n(A_{2,n})-p_n.
\tag{17}
\]
The map (16) is invertible, maps halfspaces bijectively to halfspaces, and
is a translation on each slice.  Hence the full transformation multiplies
every slice area and every halfspace depth by the same number \(d_n\).
It also preserves (9), because
\[
\frac{K_{0,n}+K_{2,n}}2
=\frac{J_n(A_{0,n})+J_n(A_{2,n})}2
\subseteq K_{1,n}.
\tag{18}
\]
This audits all affine, translation, and height-shear operations.

By construction \(0\in K_{2,n}\).  Equations (15) and (18) therefore give
\[
\frac12K_{0,n}\subseteq K_{1,n},
\qquad K_{0,n}\subseteq4B.
\tag{19}
\]
Fix any \(a_n\in K_{0,n}\).  Then \(|a_n|\le4\), and a second use of
(18) gives
\[
K_{2,n}\subseteq2K_{1,n}-a_n\subseteq8B.
\tag{20}
\]
Thus all three bodies are uniformly bounded, including the small endpoint.

Their areas are
\[
|K_{0,n}|=d_n,\qquad
|K_{1,n}|=d_ny_n,\qquad
|K_{2,n}|=d_nx_n.
\tag{21}
\]
Equation (15) implies
\[
\pi\le d_ny_n\le4\pi.
\tag{22}
\]
Using (1), pass to a subsequence on which \(d_n\to d\) for some
\(d\in[\pi,4\pi]\).  Blaschke selection, (3), and a diagonal subsequence
give (4).  Area continuity and (21) give
\[
|T_0|=|T_1|=d>0,\qquad |L|=0.
\tag{23}
\]
Therefore \(T_0,T_1\) are two-dimensional, while the nonempty compact
convex set \(L\), which contains the origin, is a point or a segment.
Minkowski addition is continuous for Hausdorff convergence, so (18)
passes to the limit and proves (5).

It remains to identify \(T_0,T_1\).  Because the original triple is a
counterexample, every point has depth strictly less than \(t_n\).  The
endpoint identity in accepted fact `949ce2fdf2e1e240` yields
\[
\delta(A_{0,n})<t_n.
\tag{24}
\]
For a middle point, the exact profile formula in the same accepted fact is
a planar \(A_{1,n}\)-cap plus two nonnegative endpoint caps.  Hence
\[
h_S((1,z))\ge d_{A_{1,n}}(z)
\]
and consequently
\[
\delta(A_{1,n})<t_n.
\tag{25}
\]
Combining (11), (24), and (25) gives
\[
\frac49
\le\frac{\delta(A_{0,n})}{|A_{0,n}|}
<\frac{2(1+x_n+y_n)}9
\longrightarrow\frac49,
\tag{26}
\]
\[
\frac49
\le\frac{\delta(A_{1,n})}{|A_{1,n}|}
<\frac{2(1+x_n+y_n)}{9y_n}
\longrightarrow\frac49.
\tag{27}
\]
The ratios in (26)-(27) are unchanged by \(J_n\), translations, and the
height shear.  Equations (14), (23), (26), and (27) therefore give
\[
\delta(T_0)=\frac49|T_0|,\qquad
\delta(T_1)=\frac49|T_1|.
\tag{28}
\]
The equality rigidity (12) proves that both limits are nondegenerate
triangles.  Notice that this argument uses exact equality only after taking
the compact Hausdorff limit.  It does not assume an unproved quantitative
stability version of Grünbaum.

We finally prove the certificate-line assertion.  Apply accepted fact
`d2d117bcc58a1ba0` at level \(t_n\), and transform the resulting
certificate by \(J_n\) and (16).  The three middle open halfplanes cover
the entire middle plane, and each full three-slice halfspace has transformed
mass strictly below \(d_nt_n\).  Thus, with (6),
\[
K_{1,n}\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^3 C_{j,n},\qquad
|C_{j,n}|<d_nt_n.
\tag{29}
\]
Put
\[
\theta_n=\frac{d_nt_n}{|K_{1,n}|}
=\frac{2(1+x_n+y_n)}{9y_n}\longrightarrow\frac49.
\tag{30}
\]
For \(\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}\), (29) also gives the uniform lower bound
\[
|C_{i,n}|
\ge |K_{1,n}|-|C_{j,n}|-|C_{k,n}|
>(1-2\theta_n)|K_{1,n}|.
\tag{31}
\]
For all large \(n\), both \(C_{i,n}\) and its complement in \(K_{1,n}\)
have positive area.  Hence every cutting line meets
\(\operatorname{int}K_{1,n}\).  Normalize its inward normal to have norm
one as in (6).  Since \(K_{1,n}\subseteq2B\), this gives
\[
|r_{i,n}|\le2.
\tag{32}
\]
After a subsequence and a permutation of the three indices,
\[
q_{i,n}\longrightarrow q_i,\qquad r_{i,n}\longrightarrow r_i.
\tag{33}
\]

Let
\[
\widehat C_i=T_1\cap\{w:q_i\cdot w\le r_i\}.
\]
The same null-line dominated-convergence argument used in (14), together
with (29)-(30), gives
\[
|\widehat C_i|\le\frac49|T_1|.
\tag{34}
\]
The three limiting closed halfplanes cover the whole plane.  Indeed, if a
point violated all three limiting weak inequalities, it would violate all
three finite-stage strict inequalities for large \(n\), contradicting the
strict open cover supplied by the certificate.

Let \(g\) be the centroid of \(T_1\).  At least one limiting halfplane
contains \(g\).  Any one that contains \(g\) must have \(g\) on its
boundary.  Otherwise, translate its boundary parallel toward \(g\).  The
smaller centroidal halfplane has area at least \(4|T_1|/9\) by
`2f9be517307232c7`, while the nonempty intervening strip has positive
area, contradicting (34).  Therefore every limiting halfplane containing
\(g\) is a centroidal equality cap.  By (12)-(13), or by the direct
triangle calculation in `43e70f89f224c5d7`, it is one of the three
centroidal vertex caps of \(T_1\).

In fact all three limiting halfplanes contain \(g\).  A limiting closed
halfplane not containing \(g\) misses a whole neighborhood of \(g\).
Consequently the equality caps that do contain \(g\) must cover a
neighborhood of \(g\).  One closed halfplane through \(g\) cannot do so.
Two can do so only if they are complementary halfplanes with the same
boundary line.  Two centroidal vertex caps of a nondegenerate triangle are
never complementary: distinct vertex caps have lines parallel to distinct
edges, while the halfplane complementary to one vertex cap has area
\(5|T_1|/9\), not \(4|T_1|/9\).  Hence all three contain \(g\).
The same argument rules out a repeated vertex cap.  Thus the three limits
are exactly the three distinct caps in (8), proving (7).

Every subsequential limit of the unordered line triple has just been shown
to be the same unordered centroidal triple.  Compactness of the line
parameters then promotes this to convergence of the full certificate
sequence as an unordered triple.  Relabeling gives the ordered convergence
in (7).  Since the finite-stage certificate was arbitrary, this proves the
claim for every associated normalized minimal certificate.

The point-or-segment alternative for \(L\) is sharp at the level of the
pinch assumptions and compatibility.  To see this exactly, let
\[
\Delta=\operatorname{conv}\{(-1,0),(1,0),(0,1)\},
\qquad |\Delta|=1,
\]
and, for \(n\ge12\), let
\[
R_n=[-1/4,1/4]\times[1/3-1/n,1/3+1/n].
\tag{35}
\]
Then \(R_n\subseteq\Delta\), \(|R_n|=1/n\), and
\[
\frac{\Delta+R_n}{2}\subseteq\Delta.
\]
Thus \(A_{0,n}=A_{1,n}=\Delta,\ A_{2,n}=R_n\) is a compatible
two-dimensional polytopal triple with \((x_n,y_n)=(1/n,1)\), an area pair
in the exact residual band of accepted fact `d1498a01819203fa`.  The
convex hull of the three displayed sections realizes it as a compact
three-slice polytope.  Taking \(p_n=(0,1/3)\) in (16) makes
\[
R_n-p_n\longrightarrow[-1/4,1/4]\times\{0\}.
\tag{36}
\]
This example is not asserted to be a counterexample.  It shows that area
pinching, compatibility, and the allowed gauges alone do not improve the
rigorous conclusion \(L=\) point or segment to \(L=\) point.
