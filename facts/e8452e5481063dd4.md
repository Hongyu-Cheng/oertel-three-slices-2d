## Statement

Let \(A_0,A_1,A_2\subseteq\mathbb R^2\) be nonempty two-dimensional convex bodies satisfying
\[
\frac{A_0+A_2}{2}\subseteq A_1.
\]
Write \(a_\ell=|A_\ell|\), \(M=\max\{a_0,a_2\}\), and \(m=\min\{a_0,a_2\}\). Let \(f_1,f_2,f_3:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) be affine, let \(s_1,s_2,s_3\in\mathbb R\), and suppose
\[
f_1+f_2+f_3=-1.
\]
For \(i=1,2,3\), define
\[
B_i=A_1\cap\{f_i\le0\},\qquad b_i=|B_i|,
\]
\[
C_{0i}=A_0\cap\{f_i-s_i\le0\},\qquad
C_{2i}=A_2\cap\{f_i+s_i\le0\},
\]
and
\[
e_i=|C_{0i}|+|C_{2i}|.
\]
Then, for every permutation \((i,j,k)\) of \((1,2,3)\),
\[
e_i+e_j\ge \min\{m,M+m-4b_k\}.
\tag{1}
\]

The hypotheses above are exactly the geometric part of the normalized certificate supplied by accepted fact `d2d117bcc58a1ba0`. Accepted fact `0b5d84877f51b7c3` additionally gives
\[
\sum_{i=1}^3 e_i\ge m,
\tag{2}
\]
while \(f_1+f_2+f_3=-1\) gives
\[
B_1\cup B_2\cup B_3=A_1,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^3b_i\ge a_1.
\tag{3}
\]
Even (1)--(3), the natural bounds \(0\le b_i\le a_1\), \(e_i\ge0\), and the full strict residual strip of accepted fact `15bb2c1680a0254e` do not imply
\[
\max_i(b_i+e_i)\ge\frac{2(M+m+a_1)}9.
\tag{4}
\]
Indeed, an exact feasible scalar survivor is
\[
a_0=M=1,\qquad a_2=m=\frac12,\qquad a_1=1,\qquad
b_1=b_2=b_3=\frac13,\qquad
e_1=e_2=e_3=\frac16.
\tag{5}
\]
If the stronger endpoint-family disjunction is retained at scalar level, set
\[
x_i=|C_{0i}|=0,\qquad y_i=|C_{2i}|=\frac16
\qquad(i=1,2,3).
\]
Then \(e_i=x_i+y_i\) and \(\sum_i y_i=a_2\).
At the area point in (5), the exact minimum of \(\max_i(b_i+e_i)\) under (1)--(3) and the natural bounds is \(1/2\), whereas the right-hand side of (4) is \(5/9\).

## Proof

The midpoint containment follows directly from the locked convex-slice setting: for \(u\in A_0\) and \(v\in A_2\), convexity of the ambient body puts
\[
\frac{(0,u)+(2,v)}2=\left(1,\frac{u+v}{2}\right)
\]
in its height-\(1\) slice, so \((u+v)/2\in A_1\).

Fix a permutation \((i,j,k)\). Define the two common strict upper endpoint sets
\[
U_0^{ij}
=A_0\cap\{f_i-s_i>0\}\cap\{f_j-s_j>0\},
\]
\[
U_2^{ij}
=A_2\cap\{f_i+s_i>0\}\cap\{f_j+s_j>0\},
\]
and put \(p_\ell=|U_\ell^{ij}|\) for \(\ell=0,2\). Because the endpoint caps use closed inequalities,
\[
U_0^{ij}=A_0\setminus(C_{0i}\cup C_{0j}),\qquad
U_2^{ij}=A_2\setminus(C_{2i}\cup C_{2j}).
\tag{6}
\]
Thus all cap-boundary points lie on the cap side of (6), and there is no boundary gap.

Suppose first that \(U_0^{ij}=\varnothing\). Then \(C_{0i}\cup C_{0j}=A_0\), so
\[
e_i+e_j\ge |C_{0i}|+|C_{0j}|\ge a_0\ge m.
\]
The same argument gives \(e_i+e_j\ge a_2\ge m\) if \(U_2^{ij}=\varnothing\). In either empty-set case, (1) follows. This includes the cases in which either cap is full. It also includes every zero-area common-upper case: an intersection of a two-dimensional convex body with finitely many open halfplanes, if nonempty, has positive area because the interior of a convex body is dense in the body.

It remains to treat the case in which both common upper sets are nonempty, hence have positive area. This case also allows either common upper set to be the full endpoint body, equivalently both associated caps there to be empty. If \(u\in U_0^{ij}\) and \(v\in U_2^{ij}\), then
\[
f_i(u)-s_i>0,\qquad f_i(v)+s_i>0,
\]
so affineness gives
\[
f_i\left(\frac{u+v}{2}\right)
=\frac{f_i(u)+f_i(v)}2>0.
\]
The identical argument gives \(f_j((u+v)/2)>0\). Since \(f_i+f_j+f_k=-1\),
\[
f_k\left(\frac{u+v}{2}\right)<-1<0.
\]
Midpoint containment therefore yields the strict inclusion
\[
\frac{U_0^{ij}+U_2^{ij}}2
\subseteq A_1\cap\{f_k<0\}\subseteq B_k.
\tag{7}
\]
Planar Brunn--Minkowski applied to the two nonempty measurable convex sets gives the stronger, unprojected inequality
\[
4b_k\ge
\left(\sqrt{p_0}+\sqrt{p_2}\right)^2
\ge p_0+p_2.
\tag{8}
\]
On the other hand, (6) and subadditivity give
\[
|C_{0i}|+|C_{0j}|\ge a_0-p_0,\qquad
|C_{2i}|+|C_{2j}|\ge a_2-p_2.
\]
Consequently,
\[
e_i+e_j\ge a_0+a_2-p_0-p_2
\ge M+m-4b_k.
\]
This implies (1) in the nonempty case. The proof did not choose an order of \(a_0,a_2\), remains valid when \(a_0=a_2\), and is symmetric in all three choices of the omitted index \(k\). It also covers \(b_k=0\): then (8) rules out the nonempty case, leaving one of the already proved empty cases.

For completeness, (3) has no boundary qualification. At every \(w\in\mathbb R^2\), at least one \(f_i(w)\) is strictly negative because their sum is \(-1\). Hence the three closed sets \(\{f_i\le0\}\) cover the plane, and finite subadditivity gives \(\sum_i b_i\ge a_1\).

We now verify the exact scalar obstruction. Normalize by \(M\) and write
\[
r=\frac mM,\qquad s=\frac{a_1}{M}.
\]
For (5), \(r=1/2\) and \(s=1\). The residual endpoint is
\[
\rho=\frac{27-2\sqrt{26}}{25},
\]
and
\[
\rho-\frac12=\frac{29-4\sqrt{26}}{50}>0
\]
because \(29^2>16\cdot26\). Since \(9/25<1/2\), the residual lower function at this point is
\[
L\left(\frac12\right)
=\frac14+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}<1,
\]
where the strict inequality is equivalent to \(9>8\). The upper function is
\[
U\left(\frac12\right)=1+\frac12=\frac32>1.
\]
Thus (5) lies strictly inside the complete residual strip asserted by accepted fact `15bb2c1680a0254e`.

The middle-cover and endpoint-payment sums in (5) are
\[
\sum_i b_i=1=a_1,\qquad
\sum_i e_i=\frac12=m.
\]
For every omitted index \(k\),
\[
\min\{m,M+m-4b_k\}
=\min\left\{\frac12,\frac32-\frac43\right\}
=\frac16,
\]
whereas \(e_i+e_j=1/3\). Hence all three inequalities (1) hold. Every row has
\[
b_i+e_i=\frac12<\frac59
=\frac{2(1+1/2+1)}9.
\]
This proves that the projected scalar system does not imply (4), even after adjoining the accepted endpoint payment (2).

It also solves the projected optimization exactly at this residual area point. For every feasible scalar tuple satisfying (2)--(3),
\[
3\max_i(b_i+e_i)
\ge\sum_i(b_i+e_i)
\ge a_1+m=\frac32,
\]
so the maximum row is at least \(1/2\). Tuple (5) attains \(1/2\) while satisfying all three pairwise ledgers, so \(1/2\) is the exact optimum.

Finally, (5) is only a scalar survivor, not a claimed geometric example. It pinpoints the body-level information lost in passing from (8) to (1). Indeed, write
\[
x_i=|C_{0i}|,\qquad y_i=|C_{2i}|.
\]
The stronger endpoint-cover alternative in accepted fact `0b5d84877f51b7c3` says that one endpoint cap family covers its whole body and that the sum of its three cap areas is at least that endpoint area. Under (5), the total cap mass is
\[
\sum_i(x_i+y_i)=\sum_i e_i=\frac12.
\]
Therefore the covered endpoint must be the body of area \(m=1/2\); its cap areas sum exactly to \(1/2\), while all three cap areas on the body of area \(M=1\) vanish. Since \(e_i=1/6\), the three caps on the smaller body each have area \(1/6\), cover that body, and have multiplicity one almost everywhere. Consequently, for every pair \((i,j)\),
\[
|U_{\mathrm{large}}^{ij}|=1,\qquad
|U_{\mathrm{small}}^{ij}|=\frac16.
\]
The full common-upper inequality (8) would then require
\[
\frac43=4b_k
\ge\left(1+\frac1{\sqrt6}\right)^2,
\]
which is false because
\[
\left(1+\frac1{\sqrt6}\right)^2-\frac43
=\frac2{\sqrt6}-\frac16>0.
\]
Thus the next genuinely joint constraint is the simultaneous unprojected system (8), with \(p_\ell\) defined as actual common-upper intersection areas and with the accepted endpoint-cover alternative retained. The projection to the row volumes \(b_i,e_i\) in (1) discards both the positive cross term \(2\sqrt{p_0p_2}\) and the common cap-overlap structure.
