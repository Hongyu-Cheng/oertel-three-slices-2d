## Statement

Let \(N=\{1,2,3\}\). Let \(f_1,f_2,f_3\) be nonconstant affine functions on \(\mathbb R^2\) whose linear parts are pairwise nonparallel and such that
\[
f_1+f_2+f_3=-1.
\]
Let \(L_1+L_2+L_3<-1\), and let \(A_0,A_2,K\subseteq\mathbb R^2\) be nonempty two-dimensional convex polytopes satisfying
\[
(A_0+A_2)/2\subseteq K.
\]
Put
\[
p_i=f_i-L_i,\qquad q_i=f_i+L_i,\qquad r_i=f_i.
\]
For \(J\subseteq N\), define the exact cells
\[
P_J=A_0\cap\{p_j\le0\ (j\in J),\ p_j>0\ (j\notin J)\},
\]
\[
Q_J=A_2\cap\{q_j\le0\ (j\in J),\ q_j>0\ (j\notin J)\},
\]
\[
R_J=K\cap\{r_j\le0\ (j\in J),\ r_j>0\ (j\notin J)\}.
\]
For disjoint \(A,B\subseteq N\), define
\[
P[A;B]=A_0\cap\{p_i\le0\ (i\in A),\ p_i>0\ (i\in B)\},
\]
and define \(Q[C;D]\) and \(R[E;F]\) analogously for disjoint pairs \(C,D\) and \(E,F\).

For \(U\subseteq V\subseteq N\), write
\[
[U,V]=\{J\subseteq N:U\subseteq J\subseteq V\},
\]
and put
\[
\mathcal P(A,B)=[A,N\setminus B]\setminus\{N\},
\]
\[
\mathcal Q(C,D)=[C,N\setminus D]\setminus\{\varnothing\},
\]
\[
\mathcal R(E,F)=[E,N\setminus F]\setminus\{\varnothing\}.
\]
Then the exact disjoint expansions are
\[
P[A;B]=\bigsqcup_{J\in\mathcal P(A,B)}P_J,\qquad
Q[C;D]=\bigsqcup_{H\in\mathcal Q(C,D)}Q_H,
\]
\[
R[E;F]=\bigsqcup_{G\in\mathcal R(E,F)}R_G.
\]
Every partial set is convex. Independently of the locations of the polytopes, the complete forced-empty classification is
\[
P[A;B]\text{ is forced empty}\iff A=N,
\]
\[
Q[C;D]\text{ is forced empty}\iff D=N,
\]
\[
R[E;F]\text{ is forced empty}\iff F=N.
\]
Every other corresponding ambient partial-sign region in \(\mathbb R^2\) has nonempty interior, although its intersection with a particular polytope may still be empty.

For arbitrary disjoint \(A,B,C,D\subseteq N\), put
\[
E=A\cap C,\qquad F=B\cap D.
\]
Then
\[
\frac{P[A;B]+Q[C;D]}2\subseteq R[E;F].
\]
More precisely, define
\[
\mathcal T(A,B,C,D)
=
\left\{
G\in\mathcal R(E,F):
\begin{array}{l}
N\setminus A\not\subseteq D\cap G,\\
N\setminus D\not\subseteq A\setminus G
\end{array}
\right\}.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\mathcal T(A,B,C,D)
=
\left\{
G\ne\varnothing:
\begin{array}{l}
\exists J\in\mathcal P(A,B),\ \exists H\in\mathcal Q(C,D),\\
J\cap H\subseteq G\subseteq J\cup H
\end{array}
\right\}.
\]
The exact incidence refinement is
\[
\frac{P[A;B]+Q[C;D]}2
\subseteq
\bigsqcup_{G\in\mathcal T(A,B,C,D)}R_G
\subseteq R[E;F].
\]
Assume \(A\ne N\) and \(D\ne N\), so both source ambient regions are nonempty. Then the second inclusion removes at least one exact target cell if and only if at least one of
\[
B=\varnothing\quad\text{and}\quad N\setminus A\subseteq D,
\]
\[
C=\varnothing,\quad N\setminus D\subseteq A,\quad\text{and}\quad D\setminus B\ne\varnothing
\]
holds. The cells removed by the first plane-sum obstruction are exactly
\[
\{G\in\mathcal R(E,F):N\setminus A\subseteq D\cap G\},
\]
and those removed by the second are exactly
\[
\{G\in\mathcal R(E,F):N\setminus D\subseteq A\setminus G\}.
\]

For the strongest refinement that is itself one convex partial-sign target, define
\[
\mu(U)=\{i\in N:U=N\setminus\{i\}\},
\]
so \(\mu(U)\) is empty unless \(|U|=2\). Put
\[
B^\sharp=B\cup\mu(A),\qquad C^\sharp=C\cup\mu(D),
\]
\[
E^\sharp=A\cap C^\sharp,\qquad F^\sharp=B^\sharp\cap D,
\qquad
\widehat E^\sharp=E^\sharp\cup\mu(F^\sharp).
\]
For non-forced sources,
\[
P[A;B]=P[A;B^\sharp],\qquad
Q[C;D]=Q[C^\sharp;D],
\]
and
\[
\frac{P[A;B]+Q[C;D]}2
\subseteq
R[\widehat E^\sharp;F^\sharp]
=
R[E^\sharp;F^\sharp]
\subseteq R[E;F].
\]
Moreover,
\[
\bigcap_{G\in\mathcal T(A,B,C,D)}G=\widehat E^\sharp,
\qquad
N\setminus\bigcup_{G\in\mathcal T(A,B,C,D)}G=F^\sharp.
\]
Thus no further coordinate can universally be added to either sign list of a single partial-sign target.

As an ambient sign-region inclusion, the last inclusion is proper exactly when, for some \(i\in N\), at least one of
\[
A=N\setminus\{i\}\quad\text{and}\quad i\in D\setminus B,
\]
\[
D=N\setminus\{i\},\quad C=\varnothing,\quad i\in A,\quad\text{and}\quad B\ne D
\]
holds. The sharper exact-cell union indexed by \(\mathcal T\) can be proper even when no stronger single partial-sign target exists.

All incidence claims are sharp: for every \(G\in\mathcal T(A,B,C,D)\), and for every fixed affine data \(f_i,L_i\) satisfying the hypotheses, there are admissible two-dimensional polytopes \(A_0,A_2,K\) and points \(x\in P[A;B]\), \(y\in Q[C;D]\) such that \((x+y)/2\) lies strictly inside the ambient exact \(R_G\)-chamber. Hence no cell in \(\mathcal T\) can be removed universally.

The convention is always \(\le0\) on an indexed coordinate and \(>0\) on an unindexed coordinate. It makes every exact-cell expansion disjoint and assigns a point with zero coordinate to the indexed cell. Every cutting set \(p_i=0\), \(q_i=0\), or \(r_i=0\) is a line, hence has planar area zero inside a two-dimensional polytope. Therefore all displayed disjoint unions are area-additive, and reversing the weak/strict boundary assignment does not change any planar area, although the stated convention is used for the exact set inclusions.

## Proof

Write
\[
s=L_1+L_2+L_3,\qquad \delta=-1-s>0.
\]
The three coordinate planes are
\[
\sum_{i=1}^3p_i=\delta,\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^3q_i=-\delta-2,\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^3r_i=-1.
\tag{1}
\]
The affine map
\[
x\longmapsto(f_1(x),f_2(x),f_3(x))
\]
is an affine isomorphism from \(\mathbb R^2\) to the plane of coordinate sum \(-1\): its image is contained in that plane, and two pairwise nonparallel linear parts are linearly independent. Thus feasibility of a strict sign pattern on any of the three planes is equivalent to feasibility of the corresponding ambient chamber in \(\mathbb R^2\).

Every point has a unique nonpositive-coordinate index set. Hence imposing only the signs in \(A,B\) gives exactly the Boolean interval
\([A,N\setminus B]\), and similarly for the other two arrangements. Equation (1) excludes \(J=N\) for \(p\), \(H=\varnothing\) for \(q\), and \(G=\varnothing\) for \(r\). This proves the three disjoint expansions.

If \(A=N\), all \(p_i\le0\), contradicting \(\sum p_i=\delta>0\). Conversely, if \(A\ne N\), choose
\[
p_i=-1\quad(i\in A),\qquad
p_i=\frac{\delta+|A|}{3-|A|}\quad(i\notin A).
\]
This lies strictly in the required ambient region, since \(B\subseteq N\setminus A\). Thus \(A=N\) is the only forced-empty \(P\)-case.

If \(D=N\), all \(q_i>0\), contradicting the second equation in (1). If \(D\ne N\), choose
\[
q_i=1\quad(i\in D),\qquad
q_i=-\frac{\delta+2+|D|}{3-|D|}\quad(i\notin D).
\]
Since \(C\subseteq N\setminus D\), this lies strictly in the required \(Q\)-region. The proof for \(R\) is identical: \(F=N\) contradicts \(\sum r_i=-1\), while for \(F\ne N\) the choice
\[
r_i=1\quad(i\in F),\qquad
r_i=-\frac{1+|F|}{3-|F|}\quad(i\notin F)
\]
lies strictly in the required region. This proves all forced-empty and ambient-interior assertions.

Each partial set is the intersection of its polytope with finitely many open or closed affine halfspaces, so it is convex. In particular its exact-cell union, although written as a disjoint Boolean union, is convex. Minkowski averages of the two source partial sets are also convex.

Take \(x\in P[A;B]\), \(y\in Q[C;D]\), and \(z=(x+y)/2\). Compatibility gives \(z\in K\), and affinity gives
\[
2r_i(z)=p_i(x)+q_i(y).
\tag{2}
\]
If \(i\in A\cap C\), both terms on the right are nonpositive, so \(r_i(z)\le0\). If \(i\in B\cap D\), both are strictly positive, so \(r_i(z)>0\). This proves
\[
z\in R[A\cap C;B\cap D].
\]

Let
\[
J=\{i:p_i(x)\le0\},\qquad
H=\{i:q_i(y)\le0\},\qquad
G=\{i:r_i(z)\le0\}.
\]
Then \(J\in\mathcal P(A,B)\), \(H\in\mathcal Q(C,D)\), and (2) gives
\[
J\cap H\subseteq G\subseteq J\cup H.
\tag{3}
\]
Also \(G\ne\varnothing\) by \(\sum r_i=-1\). This proves the existential description of \(\mathcal T\) in one direction.

Fix \(G\in\mathcal R(E,F)\). Temporarily ignore the restrictions \(J\ne N\) and \(H\ne\varnothing\). At coordinate \(i\), if \(i\in G\), the admissible membership pairs
\[
(\mathbf1_{i\in J},\mathbf1_{i\in H})
\]
are \((1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\); if \(i\notin G\), they are
\((0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\). After also imposing membership in \(A,B,C,D\), a local choice exists at every coordinate exactly because
\[
A\cap C\subseteq G,\qquad G\cap(B\cap D)=\varnothing.
\]

At coordinate \(i\), a choice with \(i\notin J\) exists exactly when
\[
i\notin A\quad\text{and}\quad(i\notin G\ \text{or}\ i\notin D).
\]
Thus some coordinate can witness \(J\ne N\) exactly when
\[
N\setminus A\not\subseteq D\cap G.
\tag{4}
\]
Likewise, a choice with \(i\in H\) exists exactly when
\[
i\notin D\quad\text{and}\quad(i\in G\ \text{or}\ i\notin A),
\]
so some coordinate can witness \(H\ne\varnothing\) exactly when
\[
N\setminus D\not\subseteq A\setminus G.
\tag{5}
\]
If the witnesses in (4) and (5) are distinct, choose their local pairs independently. If they coincide, the pair \((0,1)\) is admissible there. Fill all remaining coordinates with arbitrary admissible local pairs. Hence (4) and (5) are jointly necessary and sufficient, proving the closed formula for \(\mathcal T\).

The first obstruction has a member \(G\in\mathcal R(E,F)\) precisely when
\[
B=\varnothing,\qquad N\setminus A\subseteq D.
\tag{6}
\]
Indeed, if \(N\setminus A\subseteq D\cap G\), then every element of \(B\), which lies in \(N\setminus A\), would lie in both \(G\) and \(F=B\cap D\); hence \(B=\varnothing\). Conversely, under (6), \(G=N\) belongs to \(\mathcal R(E,F)\) and has the forbidden property.

For the second obstruction, \(N\setminus D\subseteq A\setminus G\) first forces \(N\setminus D\subseteq A\). Since \(C\subseteq N\setminus D\), while \(A\cap C\subseteq G\), it also forces \(C=\varnothing\). Moreover \(B\subseteq D\), and a nonempty admissible \(G\subseteq D\setminus B\) exists exactly when \(D\setminus B\ne\varnothing\). Conversely, any singleton in \(D\setminus B\) supplies such a \(G\). This proves the exact strictness classification.

The same obstructions can be read directly from (1) and (2). If
\(N\setminus A\subseteq D\cap G\), then \(p_i\le0\) on \(A\), while for
\(i\notin A\) one has \(q_i>0\) and \(r_i\le0\), hence
\[
p_i=2r_i-q_i<0.
\]
Thus all \(p_i\le0\), contradicting \(\sum p_i=\delta\). If
\(N\setminus D\subseteq A\setminus G\), then \(q_i>0\) on \(D\), while for
\(i\notin D\) one has \(p_i\le0\) and \(r_i>0\), hence
\[
q_i=2r_i-p_i>0.
\]
Thus all \(q_i>0\), contradicting \(\sum q_i=-\delta-2\).

It remains to identify the strongest single partial-sign target. If
\(A=N\setminus\{i\}\), then on \(P[A;B]\),
\[
p_i=\delta-\sum_{j\in A}p_j\ge\delta>0.
\]
This proves \(P[A;B]=P[A;B^\sharp]\). If
\(D=N\setminus\{i\}\), then on \(Q[C;D]\),
\[
q_i=-\delta-2-\sum_{j\in D}q_j<0,
\]
which proves \(Q[C;D]=Q[C^\sharp;D]\). Applying (2) to these strengthened source sign lists gives
\[
\frac{P[A;B]+Q[C;D]}2\subseteq R[E^\sharp;F^\sharp].
\]
Finally, if \(F^\sharp=N\setminus\{i\}\), then on this target
\[
r_i=-1-\sum_{j\in F^\sharp}r_j<-1<0.
\]
Therefore
\[
R[E^\sharp;F^\sharp]=R[\widehat E^\sharp;F^\sharp].
\]

Every \(i\in F^\sharp\) is positive in every target pattern, and every
\(i\in\widehat E^\sharp\) is nonpositive in every target pattern, so
\[
\widehat E^\sharp\subseteq\bigcap_{G\in\mathcal T}G,\qquad
F^\sharp\subseteq N\setminus\bigcup_{G\in\mathcal T}G.
\tag{7}
\]
For the reverse positive-sign assertion, let \(i\notin F^\sharp\). If \(i\notin D\), choose
\[
J=A,\qquad H=C\cup\{i\},\qquad G=J\cup H.
\]
If \(i\in D\), then \(i\notin B^\sharp\), so
\[
J=A\cup\{i\}\ne N,\qquad H=N\setminus D,\qquad G=J\cup H
\]
is valid. In both cases \(G\in\mathcal T\) and \(i\in G\).

For the reverse nonpositive-sign assertion, let \(i\notin\widehat E^\sharp\). If \(i\notin A\), take \(J=A\) and \(H=N\setminus D\). Unless \(A=\varnothing\) and \(H=\{i\}\), the set \((J\cup H)\setminus\{i\}\) contains some \(k\); then
\[
G=(J\cap H)\cup\{k\}
\]
belongs to \(\mathcal T\) and excludes \(i\). In the exceptional case,
\(D=N\setminus\{i\}\). Since \(i\notin\widehat E^\sharp\), one cannot have
\(B=D\); choose \(k\in D\setminus B\), and use
\[
J=\{k\},\qquad H=\{i\},\qquad G=\{k\}.
\]
If instead \(i\in A\), then \(i\notin C^\sharp\). Hence \(i\notin C\) and
\(D\ne N\setminus\{i\}\). There is a nonempty \(H\in\mathcal Q(C,D)\) excluding \(i\): take \(H=C\) if \(C\ne\varnothing\), and otherwise take \(H=\{k\}\) for some
\(k\in N\setminus(D\cup\{i\})\). With \(J=A\) and \(G=H\), one again gets
\(G\in\mathcal T\) and \(i\notin G\). These constructions reverse both inclusions in (7), proving the two coordinate-envelope identities.

The unstrengthened target already satisfies
\[
R[E;F]=R[E\cup\mu(F);F].
\]
Comparing this normalized pair with
\((\widehat E^\sharp,F^\sharp)\) shows that the strongest single partial target is proper exactly when
\[
F^\sharp\ne F
\quad\text{or}\quad
\widehat E^\sharp\ne E\cup\mu(F).
\]
Expanding the definitions gives precisely the two stated alternatives:
\[
A=N\setminus\{i\},\ i\in D\setminus B,
\]
or
\[
D=N\setminus\{i\},\ C=\varnothing,\ i\in A,\ B\ne D.
\]

Finally, let \(G\in\mathcal T(A,B,C,D)\). The Boolean characterization supplies
\(J\in\mathcal P(A,B)\) and \(H\in\mathcal Q(C,D)\) satisfying (3).
Accepted fact `0e51a47a9add571f` proves that every such exact incidence can be realized strictly, for the fixed affine data, by sufficiently small admissible two-dimensional polytopes around suitable source points and their midpoint. Those points lie in the required partial source sets. Thus every \(G\in\mathcal T\) is genuinely realizable, while the preceding argument proves that no \(G\notin\mathcal T\) can occur. This establishes sharpness of both the exact-cell refinement and its coordinate envelope.

Each zero set \(p_i=0\), \(q_i=0\), or \(r_i=0\) is a line because \(f_i\) is nonconstant. Its intersection with a two-dimensional polytope has planar area zero. The \(\le0\)/\(>0\) convention gives literal disjointness and assigns zero to the indexed side, while any alternative assignment changes only null sets. This proves all boundary assertions.
