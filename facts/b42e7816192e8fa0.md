## Statement

Let
\[
\kappa=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&a&b\\
c&0&d\\
e&f&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad a,b,c,d,e,f>0,
\]
and suppose
\[
\delta:=\det(I-\kappa)
=1-ac-be-df-ade-bcf>0.
\]
Define
\[
B_1=\frac1{(1+a)(1+b)},\qquad
B_2=\frac1{(1+c)(1+d)},\qquad
B_3=\frac1{(1+e)(1+f)}.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{\ \max_{1\leq i<j\leq3}(B_i+B_j)>\frac89.\ }
\tag{1}
\]
The constant \(8/9\) is best possible.

Consequently, if
\[
S=B_1+B_2+B_3,\qquad P_k=S-B_k,
\]
and
\[
\theta(S)=
\begin{cases}
8/9,&S\leq4/3,\\
(4-S)/3,&S\geq4/3,
\end{cases}
\]
then there is an index \(k\) for which
\[
P_k>\frac89\geq\theta(S).
\tag{2}
\]
In particular \(\{k:P_k\geq\theta(S)\}\ne\varnothing\), including at
\(S=4/3\).

## Proof

We first prove a two-variable estimate.  Let \(p,q,u,v>0\) satisfy
\[
pu+qv<1,
\tag{3}
\]
and put
\[
X=p(1+u),\qquad Y=q(1+v),\qquad L=1+p+q.
\]
Then \(X+Y<L\).

If \(|p-q|\leq1\), the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality gives
\[
XY\leq\frac{(X+Y)^2}{4}<\frac{L^2}{4}.
\]
Therefore
\[
\frac1{(1+u)(1+v)}
=\frac{pq}{XY}
>\frac{4pq}{(1+p+q)^2}.
\tag{4}
\]

If \(p\geq1+q\), then \(p\geq L/2\) and \(X>p\).  Since
\(Y<L-X\), and \(x(L-x)\) is nonincreasing for \(x\geq L/2\),
\[
XY<X(L-X)\leq p(L-p)=p(1+q).
\]
It follows that
\[
\frac1{(1+u)(1+v)}
=\frac{pq}{XY}
>\frac{q}{1+q}.
\tag{5}
\]
The case \(q\geq1+p\) is symmetric and gives
\[
\frac1{(1+u)(1+v)}>\frac{p}{1+p}.
\tag{6}
\]

We now obtain three inequalities of the form (3) from \(\delta>0\).
The latter condition implies
\[
1-ac>0,\qquad 1-be>0,\qquad 1-df>0.
\]
Moreover,
\[
(I-\kappa)^{-1}
=\frac1\delta
\begin{pmatrix}
1-df&a+bf&ad+b\\
c+de&1-be&d+bc\\
e+cf&f+ae&1-ac
\end{pmatrix}
\tag{7}
\]
has strictly positive entries.  Define
\[
\begin{pmatrix}t_1\\t_2\\t_3\end{pmatrix}
=(I-\kappa)^{-1}
\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Then \(t_1,t_2,t_3>0\), while
\((I-\kappa)t=\mathbf1\) gives the strict inequalities
\[
at_2+bt_3<t_1,\qquad
ct_1+dt_3<t_2,\qquad
et_1+ft_2<t_3.
\tag{8}
\]

The claim is invariant under a simultaneous permutation of the three
indices.  Relabel them so that
\[
t_1\geq t_2\geq t_3.
\tag{9}
\]
First suppose that
\[
t_1\leq t_2+t_3.
\tag{10}
\]
Then \(t_1,t_2,t_3\) satisfy all three triangle inequalities.  For row
\(i\), divide the corresponding inequality in (8) by \(t_i\), and
apply (4) with
\[
p=\frac{t_j}{t_i},\qquad q=\frac{t_k}{t_i},
\]
where \(\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}\).  The condition \(|p-q|\leq1\) is exactly
\(|t_j-t_k|\leq t_i\).  Hence
\[
B_i>\frac{4t_jt_k}{(t_1+t_2+t_3)^2}
\qquad(i=1,2,3).
\tag{11}
\]
In particular, with
\[
x=\frac{t_1}{t_1+t_2+t_3},
\]
equations (9)-(10) give \(1/3\leq x\leq1/2\), and
\[
\begin{aligned}
B_2+B_3
&>\frac{4t_1(t_2+t_3)}
{(t_1+t_2+t_3)^2}\\
&=4x(1-x)
=\frac89+4\left(x-\frac13\right)\left(\frac23-x\right)
\geq\frac89.
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
\]

It remains to consider
\[
t_1\geq t_2+t_3.
\tag{13}
\]
For the second inequality in (8), take
\[
p=\frac{t_1}{t_2},\qquad q=\frac{t_3}{t_2}.
\]
Condition (13) says \(p\geq1+q\), so (5) yields
\[
B_2>\frac{t_3}{t_2+t_3}.
\tag{14}
\]
Applying the same argument to the third inequality in (8) gives
\[
B_3>\frac{t_2}{t_2+t_3}.
\tag{15}
\]
Thus \(B_2+B_3>1>8/9\).  Together with (12), this proves (1).

To see sharpness, take
\[
a=b=c=d=e=f=r,\qquad 0<r<\frac12.
\]
Then
\[
\delta=1-3r^2-2r^3=(1-2r)(1+r)^2>0
\]
and
\[
B_1=B_2=B_3=\frac1{(1+r)^2}.
\]
As \(r\uparrow1/2\), every pair sum decreases to \(8/9\).  Hence no
larger universal constant can replace \(8/9\) in (1).

Finally, choose \(k\) for which \(B_k\) is smallest.  Then \(P_k\) is
the sum of the two largest \(B_i\), so (1) gives \(P_k>8/9\).  If
\(S\leq4/3\), this is \(P_k>\theta(S)\).  If \(S\geq4/3\), then
\[
\theta(S)=\frac{4-S}{3}\leq\frac89<P_k.
\]
This proves (2) and the asserted nonemptiness.
