## Statement

Let \(P,Q\subset\mathbb R^2\) be nonempty two-dimensional convex polytopes with areas
\[
|P|=M\ge m=|Q|>0.
\]
Let \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) be a nonconstant affine function, let \(c\ge0\) and \(0<t<M\), and define
\[
F_P(a)=|P\cap\{f\le a\}|,\qquad
F_Q(a)=|Q\cap\{f\le a\}|,
\]
\[
\mu(a)=c+F_P(a)+F_Q(-a),\qquad
I=\{a\in\mathbb R:\mu(a)<t\}.
\]
Then \(\mu\) is continuous and polynomial of degree at most two on every cell of a finite partition of \(\mathbb R\). If
\[
\alpha_R=\min_R f,\qquad \beta_R=\max_R f
\]
for \(R=P,Q\), then
\[
\mu(a)=c+m\quad\text{for }a\le\min\{\alpha_P,-\beta_Q\},
\]
and
\[
\mu(a)=c+M\quad\text{for }a\ge\max\{\beta_P,-\alpha_Q\}.
\]
Moreover, \(I\) is an open finite union of intervals, some of which may be rays.

Assume in addition that \(c+m\ge t\) and \(I\ne\varnothing\). Then \(I\) is bounded, \(L:=\inf I\) is finite, and
\[
\mu(L)=t.
\]
Classify a lower cap as empty, proper, or full according as its two-dimensional area is zero, strictly between zero and the ambient polygon's area, or equal to that area. At \(L\), the \(P\)-cap \(P\cap\{f\le L\}\) is never full, and the \(Q\)-cap \(Q\cap\{f\le-L\}\) is never empty. More precisely:

(i) If \(c+m>t\), the possible ordered cap states are exactly
\[
(\text{empty},\text{proper})
\quad\text{and}\quad
(\text{proper},\text{proper}).
\]

(ii) If \(c+m=t\), the possible ordered cap states are exactly
\[
(\text{empty},\text{full})
\quad\text{and}\quad
(\text{proper},\text{proper}).
\]
In the empty-full case one necessarily has
\[
L=-\beta_Q\le\alpha_P.
\]

For a closed lower cap, the empty area state means that its threshold is at or below the minimum support value. It is literally empty below that value and is the minimizing face at equality. All alternatives above occur for rectangles, and both versions of the empty \(P\)-cap occur.

## Proof

Choose an affine function \(g\) whose linear part is independent of the linear part of \(f\). The map
\[
\Phi(x)=(f(x),g(x))
\]
is an affine bijection. For either polygon \(R=P,Q\), let \(\lambda_R(s)\) be the one-dimensional length of the vertical section of \(\Phi(R)\) at first coordinate \(s\). For a fixed constant \(J>0\), the change-of-variables formula gives
\[
F_R(a)=J\int_{-\infty}^{a}\lambda_R(s)\,ds.
\]
Between two consecutive first-coordinate values of the vertices of \(\Phi(R)\), the upper and lower endpoints of the vertical section lie on fixed polygon edges. Each endpoint is affine in \(s\), so \(\lambda_R(s)\) is affine there. Hence \(F_R\) is polynomial of degree at most two between consecutive projected vertex values. There are finitely many such values. The integral representation also proves continuity at every breakpoint, including projected vertical edges.

The section length is zero outside \([\alpha_R,\beta_R]\) and positive on its interior. Consequently,
\[
F_R(s)=0\iff s\le\alpha_R,\qquad
0<F_R(s)<|R|\iff \alpha_R<s<\beta_R,
\]
and
\[
F_R(s)=|R|\iff s\ge\beta_R.
\]
At \(s=\alpha_R\), the closed cap is the minimizing face and has area zero. If \(s<\alpha_R\), it is literally empty.

A common refinement of the projected vertex values of \(P\) and the negatives of those of \(Q\) is a finite partition on each cell of which
\[
\mu(a)=c+F_P(a)+F_Q(-a)
\]
is quadratic or lower degree. The support identities above give the two asserted constant tails. Continuity implies that \(I\) is open. On each partition cell, a strict quadratic inequality has finitely many interval components, with the identically positive, zero, and negative cases causing no difficulty. Thus \(I\) is a finite union of open intervals and rays.

Now suppose \(c+m\ge t\) and \(I\ne\varnothing\). The left tail has value at least \(t\), while the right tail has value
\[
c+M>t
\]
because \(c\ge0\) and \(t<M\). Therefore \(I\) is bounded and nonempty, so \(L=\inf I\in\mathbb R\).

If \(\mu(L)<t\), continuity would put points smaller than \(L\) in \(I\), contradicting the definition of \(L\). Hence \(\mu(L)\ge t\). For every \(n\), the definition of the infimum gives \(a_n\in I\) with
\[
L<a_n<L+\frac1n.
\]
Continuity then gives \(\mu(L)\le t\). Therefore \(\mu(L)=t\).

Put
\[
U=F_P(L),\qquad V=F_Q(-L),\qquad d=t-c.
\]
Since \(\mu\ge c\) and \(I\ne\varnothing\), one has \(c<t\), so \(d>0\). Also
\[
d\le m,\qquad d<M,\qquad U+V=d.
\]
The \(P\)-cap cannot be full, since \(U=M\) would imply \(d\ge M\).

The \(Q\)-cap cannot be empty. Indeed, if \(V=0\), then for every \(a\ge L\),
\[
F_Q(-a)\le F_Q(-L)=0
\]
and
\[
F_P(a)\ge F_P(L).
\]
Thus \(\mu(a)\ge\mu(L)=t\) for every \(a\ge L\). Every point of \(I\) is at least \(L\), contradicting \(I\ne\varnothing\). Hence \(V>0\).

If \(c+m>t\), then \(d<m\). Therefore
\[
0<V\le d<m,
\]
so the \(Q\)-cap is proper. Also \(0\le U< M\), giving exactly the empty-proper and proper-proper alternatives.

If \(c+m=t\), then \(d=m\). The equality \(U+V=m\), together with \(V>0\), gives two cases. If \(U=0\), then \(V=m\), so the caps are empty-full. Conversely, a full \(Q\)-cap forces \(U=0\). If \(U>0\), then
\[
0<U<m<M,\qquad 0<V=m-U<m,
\]
so both caps are proper.

It remains to prove the support relation in the empty-full equality case. The identities \(U=0\) and \(V=m\) give
\[
L\le\alpha_P,\qquad L\le-\beta_Q.
\]
If \(L<-\beta_Q\), then throughout some interval immediately to the right of \(L\), the \(Q\)-cap remains full. On that interval,
\[
\mu(a)=c+m+F_P(a)\ge t,
\]
contradicting the existence of points of \(I\) arbitrarily close to \(L\) from the right. Hence
\[
L=-\beta_Q\le\alpha_P.
\]

The classification is sharp. Take \(f(x,y)=x\) and
\[
Q=[-1,0]\times[0,1].
\]
Then \(m=1\) and
\[
F_Q(-a)=
\begin{cases}
1,&a\le0,\\
1-a,&0\le a\le1,\\
0,&a\ge1.
\end{cases}
\]

For the strict empty-proper case, take
\[
P=[10,12]\times[0,1],\qquad c=0,\qquad t=\frac12.
\]
Here \(M=2\),
\[
I=\left(\frac12,\frac{21}{2}\right),\qquad L=\frac12,
\]
and
\[
F_P(L)=0,\qquad F_Q(-L)=\frac12.
\]

For the strict proper-proper case, take
\[
P=[0,4]\times\left[0,\frac12\right],\qquad c=0,\qquad t=\frac34.
\]
Then
\[
I=\left(\frac12,\frac32\right),\qquad L=\frac12,
\]
and
\[
F_P(L)=\frac14,\qquad F_Q(-L)=\frac12.
\]

For the equality empty-full case, take
\[
P=[10,12]\times[0,1],\qquad c=0,\qquad t=1.
\]
Then
\[
I=(0,11),\qquad L=0,
\]
and the \(P\)-cap is literally empty while the \(Q\)-cap is all of \(Q\).

For the equality proper-proper case, take
\[
P=\left[-\frac12,\frac72\right]\times\left[0,\frac12\right],
\qquad c=0,\qquad t=1.
\]
Then
\[
I=\left(\frac12,\frac32\right),\qquad L=\frac12,
\]
and both cap areas equal \(1/2\).

Finally, the empty area state need not be literal emptiness. In the strict example, replacing \(P\) by
\[
\left[\frac12,\frac{17}{2}\right]\times\left[0,\frac14\right]
\]
gives
\[
I=\left(\frac12,\frac52\right),
\]
and the \(P\)-cap at \(L=1/2\) is its minimizing edge. In the equality example, taking
\[
P=[0,4]\times\left[0,\frac12\right],\qquad t=1
\]
gives \(I=(0,2)\), and the \(P\)-cap at \(L=0\) is again its minimizing edge. Thus the hypotheses determine exactly the cap-mass states listed above, but do not distinguish literal emptiness from a zero-area minimizing face.
