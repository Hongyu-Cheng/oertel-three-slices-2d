## Statement

Let \(\mathcal R_n\) be a sequence satisfying the complete canonical
residual-counterexample hypotheses of accepted fact
`d1177b21864bbcc6`, with
\[
q_n=1,\qquad
x_{1,n}=x_{2,n}=x_{3,n}=\frac5{21},\qquad
m_n=\frac{12}{7},\qquad
D_n\to0.
\tag{1}
\]
Put \(A_{2,n}\) in the John position of that fact.  Choose any
\(z_n\in A_{0,n}\), put \(v_n=z_n/2\), and apply
\[
(h,z)\longmapsto (h,z-(2-h)v_n).
\tag{2}
\]
Then the two families
\[
K_n-v_n,\qquad A_{0,n}-2v_n
\tag{3}
\]
are contained in fixed compact sets.

More precisely, if a subsequence of (3) had unbounded diameter, then after
a further common affine normalization of the three planar bodies, the
middle bodies would converge to a nondegenerate triangle \(K\).  The three
limiting middle lines would be the three lines through the centroid of
\(K\) parallel to its sides, with the indexed side pointing toward the
opposite vertex.  In coordinates
\[
\psi_1+\psi_2+\psi_3=0,
\]
the limit would be
\[
K=\{\psi_i\ge-1:\ i=1,2,3\}.
\tag{4}
\]
No other convex, triangular, or one-vertex-per-sector limit can satisfy
the three limiting \(4/9\) marginals.  The limit (4) itself is incompatible
with the positive \(Q\)-caps and reflected \(P\)-thresholds, so the assumed
escape cannot occur.

## Proof

First fix the gauge and derive the scalar limits without assuming them.
Write the John-normalized bodies again as \(A_{0,n},K_n,A_{2,n}\).
Accepted fact `ecee03aff61db31e` shows that (2) is an exact affine symmetry
of the three-slice problem.  After (2),
\[
0\in A_{0,n},\qquad
\frac{A_{0,n}+A_{2,n}}2\subseteq K_n.
\tag{5}
\]
Since
\[
B\subseteq A_{2,n}\subseteq2B
\]
in the John position of `d1177b21864bbcc6`, equation (5) gives
\[
\frac B2\subseteq K_n.
\tag{6}
\]
Widths are additive under Minkowski sums, so (5) also gives
\[
w_{A_{0,n}}(u)+w_{A_{2,n}}(u)
\le 2w_{K_n}(u)
\tag{7}
\]
for every unit vector \(u\).  Hence unbounded diameter of either family in
(3) forces unbounded diameter of \(K_n\).

Suppose along a subsequence that
\(\operatorname{diam}K_n\to\infty\).  Because of (6), if
\[
R_n=\max\{|z|:z\in K_n\},
\]
then the convex hull of a farthest point and the unit-length chord of
\(B/2\) perpendicular to it is contained in \(K_n\).  It has area
\(R_n/2\).  Since \(R_n\ge\operatorname{diam}(K_n)/2\),
\[
|K_n|\ge\frac14\operatorname{diam}(K_n)\to\infty.
\tag{8}
\]
The transformed central \(Q\)-cell area \(q'_n\) satisfies
\[
\frac{7\pi}{12}\le q'_n\le\frac{7\pi}{3}
\tag{9}
\]
by `d1177b21864bbcc6`.  Thus the canonical middle mass \(k_n\), normalized
by \(q_n=1\), tends to infinity.

Put
\[
p_n=p_{\varnothing,n},\qquad r_n=r_{123,n}.
\]
The canonical tails recorded in `d721506a274e22da` give
\[
0\le u_{i,n}\le\frac5{21}.
\tag{10}
\]
Every nonempty \(P\)-cell is counted at least once in
\(\sum_i u_{i,n}\), and hence
\[
0\le M_n-p_n\le\sum_i u_{i,n}\le\frac57.
\tag{11}
\]
The residual no-half-slice hypothesis used in
`6b69e5c4a97d4e32` gives both strict balances
\[
M_n<\frac{12}{7}+k_n,\qquad
k_n<M_n+\frac{12}{7}.
\tag{12}
\]
Equations (8)-(12) imply
\[
p_n\to\infty,\qquad
\frac{M_n}{p_n}\to1,\qquad
\frac{k_n}{p_n}\to1.
\tag{13}
\]

At the fixed ratios, the exact \(W=0\) and crossing identities recorded in
`d721506a274e22da` are
\[
2p_n=\frac{69}{7}+k_n+\eta_n,
\tag{14}
\]
\[
u_{i,n}+\frac{31}{21}+c_{i,n}
=\frac{2(M_n+12/7+k_n)}9.
\tag{15}
\]
Dividing (14)-(15) by \(p_n\) and using (10) and (13) gives
\[
\frac{\eta_n}{p_n}\to1,\qquad
\frac{c_{i,n}}{p_n}\to\frac49
\quad(i=1,2,3).
\tag{16}
\]
Finally, the exact definition
\[
\eta_n=w_n+3d_{R,n}+6r_n,
\qquad
0\le w_n\le\frac{10}{7},
\tag{17}
\]
is also recorded in `d721506a274e22da`.  Equations (16)-(17) give
\[
\frac{d_{R,n}+2r_n}{p_n}\to\frac13.
\tag{18}
\]
This proves every scalar limit used below.

We next record the only centroid fact needed for the blow-up.

CENTROID HALFSPACE LEMMA
Let \(C\) be a planar convex body with centroid \(g\), and let \(H\) be a
closed halfplane containing \(g\).  Then
\[
|C\cap H|\ge\frac49|C|.
\tag{19}
\]
If equality holds, then \(g\in\partial H\), \(C\) is a triangle,
\(\partial H\) is parallel to one side, and \(H\) points toward the
opposite vertex.

For completeness, take the coordinate normal to \(\partial H\), put the
centroid at \(0\), and write \(f(t)\) for the lengths of the parallel
sections of \(C\).  Convexity says that \(f\) is nonnegative and concave
on its support, while the centroid condition is
\[
\int t f(t)\,dt=0.
\tag{20}
\]
The elementary one-dimensional centroid inequality for a nonnegative
concave function is
\[
\int_0^\infty f(t)\,dt
\ge\frac49\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)\,dt.
\tag{21}
\]
One direct proof is obtained by replacing the graph successively on each
side of \(0\) by its supporting chords while preserving the two moments
in (20).  Concavity makes each replacement decrease the left side of
(21).  The terminal graph is one affine segment
\[
f(t)=c(\beta-t)\quad(\alpha\le t\le\beta),
\qquad c>0.
\]
Its centroid is
\[
\frac{2\alpha+\beta}{3}.
\]
Thus (20) gives \(\beta=-2\alpha\), and its positive mass fraction is
\[
\frac{\beta^2}{(\beta-\alpha)^2}=\frac49.
\]
Equality in the chord replacements requires the original \(f\) itself
to be affine, to vanish at the positive endpoint, and to satisfy
\(\beta=-2\alpha\).  If
\[
C=\{(t,s):\ell(t)\le s\le r(t)\},
\]
then \(r\) and \(-\ell\) are concave and
\(r-\ell=f\) is affine.  Equality in concavity therefore makes both
\(r\) and \(\ell\) affine.  Since \(f\) vanishes at one endpoint, these
two affine boundary graphs meet there, so \(C\) is a triangle.  The
section at the other endpoint is its opposite side.  This proves (19)
and its equality case for a boundary through \(g\).  If \(g\) lies in
the interior of \(H\), shifting the boundary parallel to itself until it
passes through \(g\) removes a positive-area slab, so equality in (19)
is impossible.  The centroid halfspace lemma follows.

We now normalize the alleged escape.  Apply a further invertible affine
map, common to all three bodies and all cutting lines, which puts \(K_n\)
in John position:
\[
B\subseteq K_n\subseteq2B.
\tag{22}
\]
This second normalization is only for analysis.  It preserves every cell
area ratio, compatibility, and all threshold incidences.  Pass to a
subsequence such that
\[
K_n\longrightarrow K
\tag{23}
\]
in Hausdorff distance.  The limit \(K\) is a two-dimensional convex body.

Use the middle affine functions
\[
\lambda_{i,n}=-f_{i,n},\qquad
\lambda_{1,n}+\lambda_{2,n}+\lambda_{3,n}=1,
\tag{24}
\]
so the \(i\)-th middle halfplane is
\[
H_{i,n}=\{\lambda_{i,n}\ge0\}.
\]
Put
\[
s_{i,n}=|\nabla\lambda_{i,n}|>0,\qquad
\phi_{i,n}=\frac{\lambda_{i,n}}{s_{i,n}},
\qquad
S_n=s_{1,n}+s_{2,n}+s_{3,n},
\]
\[
\alpha_{i,n}=\frac{s_{i,n}}{S_n}.
\tag{25}
\]
Thus \(\phi_{i,n}\) is the signed unit-normal equation of the same line,
and (24) becomes the positive affine dependence
\[
\sum_{i=1}^3\alpha_{i,n}\phi_{i,n}=\frac1{S_n},
\qquad
\alpha_{i,n}>0,\qquad
\sum_i\alpha_{i,n}=1.
\tag{26}
\]

By (13) and (16),
\[
\frac{|K_n\cap H_{i,n}|}{|K_n|}
=\frac{c_{i,n}}{k_n}\longrightarrow\frac49.
\tag{27}
\]
Consequently every line \(\partial H_{i,n}\) meets the fixed ball in
(22), and both sides retain positive area.  After another subsequence,
\[
\phi_{i,n}\longrightarrow\phi_i,\qquad
\alpha_{i,n}\longrightarrow\alpha_i,\qquad
\frac1{S_n}\longrightarrow c\ge0,
\tag{28}
\]
where every \(\phi_i\) is a nonconstant affine function with unit gradient,
\(\alpha_i\ge0\), and \(\sum_i\alpha_i=1\).  Hausdorff convergence and
zero area of the limiting lines give
\[
|K\cap\{\phi_i\ge0\}|=\frac49|K|
\quad(i=1,2,3),
\tag{29}
\]
\[
\sum_i\alpha_i\phi_i=c.
\tag{30}
\]

Let \(g\) be the centroid of \(K\).  If \(\phi_i(g)>0\), then the
halfplane \(\{\phi_i\ge0\}\) contains \(g\) in its interior.  Moving its
boundary to \(g\) produces a strictly smaller halfplane section containing
the centroid.  Its area is strictly less than \(4|K|/9\), contrary to
(19).  Therefore
\[
\phi_i(g)\le0\quad(i=1,2,3).
\tag{31}
\]
Evaluating (30) at \(g\) shows that \(c=0\).  Indeed, \(c>0\) would be a
positive weighted sum of the nonpositive numbers in (31).  Hence
\[
\sum_i\alpha_i\phi_i=0.
\tag{32}
\]

All three coefficients in (32) are positive.  If exactly one were
positive, (32) would make a nonconstant affine function identically zero.
If exactly two, say \(\alpha_i,\alpha_j>0\), then
\[
\alpha_i\phi_i=-\alpha_j\phi_j.
\]
The two halfplanes would be complementary up to their common boundary,
so their area fractions would sum to \(1\), whereas (29) makes their sum
\(8/9\).  Both alternatives are impossible.  Thus
\[
\alpha_1\alpha_2\alpha_3>0.
\tag{33}
\]
Equations (31)-(33) now force
\[
\phi_1(g)=\phi_2(g)=\phi_3(g)=0.
\tag{34}
\]

Apply the equality case of the centroid halfspace lemma to each equation
in (29).  The body \(K\) is a triangle, each line
\(\{\phi_i=0\}\) passes through its centroid and is parallel to one side,
and the indexed halfplane points toward the opposite vertex.  The three
lines must correspond to the three different sides.  Indeed, two copies
of the same centroidal side-parallel line have the same indexed
orientation; their two positive terms in (32) would force the third term
to have the complementary orientation on that same line, whose area is
\(5|K|/9\), contrary to (29).  In particular the lines are distinct and
concurrent at \(g\).

Let \(\psi_i\) be the canonical affine equation for the \(i\)-th one of
these lines, positive toward its opposite vertex and normalized by
\[
K=\{\psi_i\ge-1:\ i=1,2,3\}.
\tag{35}
\]
The values of \(\psi_i\) at the two vertices of the parallel side are
\(-1,-1\).  Since \(g\) is the centroid and \(\psi_i(g)=0\), its value at
the opposite vertex is \(2\).  Hence
\[
\psi_1+\psi_2+\psi_3=0.
\tag{36}
\]
There are numbers \(m_i>0\) such that
\[
\phi_i=m_i\psi_i.
\]
Substitution in (32), followed by the uniqueness of the affine dependence
in (36), gives one number \(\tau>0\) with
\[
\alpha_1m_1=\alpha_2m_2=\alpha_3m_3=\tau.
\tag{37}
\]
Equations (25), (28), and (37) give, locally uniformly,
\[
\frac{\lambda_{i,n}}{S_n\tau}
=\frac{\alpha_{i,n}\phi_{i,n}}{\tau}
\longrightarrow\psi_i.
\tag{38}
\]
Since \(c=0\) in (28), \(S_n\to\infty\).

Define the common lower offset
\[
a_n=\max_{1\le i\le3}
\left(-\min_{z\in K_n}\lambda_{i,n}(z)\right).
\tag{39}
\]
Hausdorff convergence (23) and uniform convergence (38) give
\[
\frac{a_n}{S_n\tau}\longrightarrow1.
\tag{40}
\]
Therefore
\[
K_n\subseteq
K_{a_n,n}:=
\{\lambda_{i,n}\ge-a_n:\ i=1,2,3\},
\tag{41}
\]
and, after division of its equations by \(S_n\tau\),
\[
K_{a_n,n}\longrightarrow K.
\tag{42}
\]
Thus the two areas in (23) and (42) have the same positive limit.  If
\(r_n\) denotes the central middle-cell area in the original \(q_n=1\)
normalization, the direct homothety calculation in
`e1361027c1dda6fa` gives
\[
|K_{a_n,n}|=r_n(1+3a_n)^2
\tag{43}
\]
in those scalar units.  Consequently
\[
\theta_n:=
\frac{k_n}{r_n(1+3a_n)^2}
\longrightarrow1.
\tag{44}
\]
This proves both the equal-offset classification and asymptotic full
occupancy.

For comparison, the same rigidity is visible by an exact calculation in
the smallest fan-parallel triangular class.  If
\[
K(a_1,a_2,a_3)=\{\lambda_i\ge-a_i:\ i=1,2,3\},
\qquad a_i>0,
\]
put
\[
A=1+a_1+a_2+a_3,\qquad
\mu_i=\frac{\lambda_i+a_i}{A}.
\]
Then \(\mu_i\ge0\), \(\sum_i\mu_i=1\), and uniform area on \(K\) becomes
uniform area on the unit barycentric simplex.  The middle condition
\(\lambda_i\ge0\) is \(\mu_i\ge a_i/A\).  Hence
\[
\frac{c_i}{|K|}
=\left(1-\frac{a_i}{A}\right)^2,
\tag{45}
\]
\[
\frac{|R_{\{i\}}|}{|K|}
=\frac{2a_ja_k}{A^2},
\qquad
\frac{|R_{\{i,j\}}|}{|K|}
=\frac{a_k^2+2a_k}{A^2},
\qquad
\frac{|R_{\{1,2,3\}}|}{|K|}
=\frac1{A^2}.
\tag{46}
\]
If the triangle escapes and all three marginals tend to \(4/9\), equation
(45) forces
\[
\frac{a_1}{A},\frac{a_2}{A},\frac{a_3}{A}\longrightarrow\frac13.
\tag{47}
\]
Equations (46) then give the unique limiting fractions
\[
\frac{|R_{\{i\}}|}{|K|}\to\frac29,\qquad
\frac{|R_{\{i,j\}}|}{|K|}\to\frac19,\qquad
\frac{|R_{\{1,2,3\}}|}{|K|}\to0.
\tag{48}
\]
Thus even an unequal-offset fan-parallel triangle cannot be an anisotropic
limit.  The preceding centroid argument shows that allowing arbitrary
convex polygons does not create further limits.

It remains to retain the positive \(Q\)-caps.  We prove the required
reflected-support bound directly.  At one finite stage suppress the index
\(n\).  Let the \(Q\)-thresholds in the \(\lambda_i\) coordinates be
\[
Q:\ \lambda_i\ge L_i.
\]
The exact reflection about the middle threshold gives
\[
R:\ \lambda_i\ge0,\qquad
P:\ \lambda_i\ge-L_i.
\tag{49}
\]
This is the threshold identity obtained from the definitions in
`d1177b21864bbcc6`.  Put
\[
\beta_i=-L_i,\qquad
\delta=\beta_1+\beta_2+\beta_3-1.
\tag{50}
\]
The central \(P\)-empty cell has positive area and is contained in
\[
\mathcal T_P=\{\lambda_i\le\beta_i:\ i=1,2,3\}.
\tag{51}
\]
Since \(\sum_i\lambda_i=1\), positivity of that cell implies
\(\delta>0\).

For each \(i\), the positive \(Q\)-double cap missing \(i\), whose
positivity is part of the exact \(Q\)-cell data in
`d1177b21864bbcc6`, contains a point \(y_i\in A_2\) with
\[
\lambda_i(y_i)<L_i=-\beta_i.
\tag{52}
\]
For any point \(x\) in the \(P\)-empty cell, compatibility and (41) give
\[
\frac{x+y_i}{2}\in K\subseteq K_a.
\]
Therefore
\[
\lambda_i(x)+\lambda_i(y_i)\ge-2a,
\]
and (52) yields
\[
\lambda_i(x)>\beta_i-2a.
\tag{53}
\]
This holds separately for all three indices; the cap point may depend on
\(i\).

Use the reflected barycentric coordinates
\[
\nu_i=\frac{\beta_i-\lambda_i}{\delta}.
\tag{54}
\]
On \(\mathcal T_P\), they satisfy
\[
\nu_i\ge0,\qquad \nu_1+\nu_2+\nu_3=1.
\]
Equations (51), (53), and (54) put the \(P\)-empty cell inside
\[
\left\{\nu_i\ge0,\quad
\sum_i\nu_i=1,\quad
\nu_i<\frac{2a}{\delta}\ (i=1,2,3)\right\}.
\tag{55}
\]
The affine map in (54) is a reflected dilation by factor \(\delta\) from
the central middle triangle \(\{\lambda_i\ge0\}\).  Hence
\[
|\mathcal T_P|=\delta^2r
\tag{56}
\]
in the common scalar area units.

For the unit barycentric simplex, the area fraction satisfying
\(\max_i\nu_i\le s\) is
\[
F(s)=
\begin{cases}
0,&0\le s\le1/3,\\
(3s-1)^2,&1/3\le s\le1/2,\\
1-3(1-s)^2,&1/2\le s\le1,\\
1,&s\ge1.
\end{cases}
\tag{57}
\]
For \(s\ge1/2\), this removes three disjoint vertex triangles of relative
area \((1-s)^2\).  For \(1/3\le s\le1/2\), inclusion-exclusion gives
\[
1-3(1-s)^2+3(1-2s)^2=(3s-1)^2.
\]
Thus (55)-(57) imply
\[
p\le\delta^2rF(2a/\delta).
\tag{58}
\]
The factor in (58) has the uniform exact bound
\[
\delta^2F(2a/\delta)\le6a^2.
\tag{59}
\]
Indeed, for \(\delta\le2a\) it is at most \(4a^2\).  For
\(2a\le\delta\le4a\), it equals
\[
\delta^2-3(\delta-2a)^2\le6a^2,
\]
with equality at \(\delta=3a\).  For \(4a\le\delta\le6a\), it equals
\[
(6a-\delta)^2\le4a^2.
\]
For \(\delta\ge6a\), it is zero.  Consequently,
\[
p_n\le6a_n^2r_n
\tag{60}
\]
at every stage to which (41) applies.

Finally, combine (43), (44), and (60):
\[
\frac{k_n}{p_n}
\ge
\theta_n\frac{(1+3a_n)^2}{6a_n^2}.
\tag{61}
\]
Equations (40) and \(S_n\to\infty\) give \(a_n\to\infty\), while
\(\theta_n\to1\).  Therefore
\[
\liminf_n\frac{k_n}{p_n}\ge\frac32.
\tag{62}
\]
This contradicts the independently derived scalar limit
\(k_n/p_n\to1\) in (13).  Hence the gauged middle bodies cannot have
unbounded diameter.  Equation (7) then bounds the gauged large outer
bodies as well.  This proves the statement.
