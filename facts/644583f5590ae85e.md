## Statement

Let \(v\) be a finite positive nondecreasing concave function on
\([0,1]\), with its endpoint values chosen as the corresponding
one-sided limits.  Fix \(0<\lambda<1\).  Choose one left clipping
alternative
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\mathrm{(L_i)}&
0<\alpha<\lambda,\qquad v(\alpha)=\lambda-\alpha,\\
\mathrm{(L_0)}&
\alpha=0,\qquad v(0)\geq\lambda,
\end{array}
\tag{1}
\]
and one right clipping alternative
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\mathrm{(R_i)}&
\lambda<\beta<1,\qquad v(\beta)=\beta-\lambda,\\
\mathrm{(R_1)}&
\beta=1,\qquad v(1)\geq1-\lambda.
\end{array}
\tag{2}
\]
Define
\[
\begin{aligned}
I(v)&=\int_0^1v(u)\,du,
&
R(v)&=\int_0^\lambda v(u)\,du,\\
X(v)&=\int_0^\alpha v(u)\,du,
&
T(v)&=\int_\beta^1v(u)\,du,\\
C&=\frac{(\beta-\lambda)^2-(\lambda-\alpha)^2}{2},
&
J(v)&=-X(v)+C+T(v),
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]
and
\[
A(v)=I(v)+9J(v),\qquad
K(v)=5J(v)+8R(v)-3I(v).
\tag{4}
\]
If
\[
A(v)<0,\qquad K(v)<0,
\tag{5}
\]
then there is a width
\[
\widetilde v(u)
=\widetilde a+\widetilde h\,u
{}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_i\min\{u,t_i\},
\tag{6}
\]
where
\[
\widetilde a>0,\qquad
\widetilde h\geq0,\qquad
d_i>0,\qquad
0<t_1<\cdots<t_N<1,
\]
such that:

1. \(\widetilde v\) has the same clipping points and the same endpoint
   alternatives (1)--(2);
2. \(A(\widetilde v)=A(v)\) and
   \(K(\widetilde v)=K(v)\);
3. \(N\leq3\), and \(N\leq2\) when \(\alpha=0\).

Kink atoms at \(\alpha,\lambda,\beta\) are allowed, so the statement
includes every clipping and kink-order boundary.

## Proof

We first put the curvature representation on a finite measure space.
A finite concave function on \((0,1)\) has finite one-sided endpoint
limits.  Resetting either endpoint to its limit changes no integral
in (3).  We use this canonical representative; if a width was
initially given with different isolated endpoint values, its actual
clipping alternative is recomputed from the canonical value, while
\(A\) and \(K\) are unchanged.

Let
\[
a=v(0),\qquad h=v'_-(1).
\]
For a finite nondecreasing concave function, the left derivative at
\(1\) exists and is finite; monotonicity gives \(h\geq0\).  Let
\[
\mu=-D^2v
\]
be its nonnegative distributional curvature measure on \((0,1)\).
Then
\[
v(u)=a+hu+\int_{(0,1)}\min\{u,t\}\,\mu(dt).
\tag{7}
\]
Indeed, the right side has the same distributional second derivative
as \(v\), the same value at \(0\), and the same final slope, so their
difference is the zero affine function.

Taking \(u=1\) in (7) gives
\[
\int_{(0,1)}t\,\mu(dt)=v(1)-a-h<\infty.
\tag{8}
\]
Thus
\[
\nu(dt)=t\,\mu(dt)
\tag{9}
\]
is a finite positive measure, even when \(\mu\) has infinite mass near
\(0\).  This weighted normalization is the compactness device used
below.

We record the linear data that must be preserved.  For any width
\(w\), put
\[
\mathcal A(w)
=\int_0^1w-9\int_0^\alpha w+9\int_\beta^1w,
\tag{10}
\]
\[
\mathcal K(w)
=-5\int_0^\alpha w+5\int_\beta^1w
{}+8\int_0^\lambda w-3\int_0^1w.
\tag{11}
\]
Then
\[
A(w)=\mathcal A(w)+9C,\qquad
K(w)=\mathcal K(w)+5C.
\tag{12}
\]
Let \(H_t(u)=\min\{u,t\}\), and let
\[
\rho=
\begin{cases}
\beta,&\beta<1,\\
1,&\beta=1.
\end{cases}
\]
For \(0<t<1\), define the moment vector
\[
\Phi(t)=\frac1t
\begin{cases}
\bigl(H_t(\rho),H_t(\alpha),
\mathcal A(H_t),\mathcal K(H_t)\bigr),
&\alpha>0,\\
\bigl(H_t(\rho),
\mathcal A(H_t),\mathcal K(H_t)\bigr),
&\alpha=0.
\end{cases}
\tag{13}
\]
Its dimension is
\[
m=
\begin{cases}
4,&\alpha>0,\\
3,&\alpha=0.
\end{cases}
\tag{14}
\]

Every coordinate of \(\Phi\) extends continuously to \([0,1]\).
The two elementary formulas needed for this assertion are
\[
\frac{H_t(c)}t=
\begin{cases}
1,&0<t\leq c,\\
c/t,&c\leq t<1,
\end{cases}
\tag{15}
\]
and
\[
\frac1t\int_0^cH_t(u)\,du=
\begin{cases}
c-t/2,&0<t\leq c,\\
c^2/(2t),&c\leq t<1.
\end{cases}
\tag{16}
\]
The two branches agree at \(t=c\), and their limits at \(0\) and \(1\)
are finite.  Since (10)--(11) are linear combinations of the
integrals in (16), the claim follows.

We now prove the finite atomic moment lemma in the sharp connected
form needed here.  Write
\[
\Phi=(\phi_0,\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_{m-1}).
\]
The first coordinate is strictly positive:
\[
\phi_0(t)=\frac{H_t(\rho)}t\geq\rho>0
\qquad(0\leq t\leq1),
\tag{17}
\]
with the continuous endpoint interpretation from (15).  If
\(\nu=0\), no curvature atoms are needed.  Otherwise put
\[
M_0=\int\phi_0\,d\nu>0,\qquad
\pi(dt)=\frac{\phi_0(t)}{M_0}\,\nu(dt),
\]
and define the continuous curve
\[
\Gamma(t)=
\left(
\frac{\phi_1(t)}{\phi_0(t)},\ldots,
\frac{\phi_{m-1}(t)}{\phi_0(t)}
\right)\in\mathbb R^{m-1}.
\tag{18}
\]
Thus \(\pi\) is a probability measure on the compact interval and
\[
y:=\int\Gamma\,d\pi
\]
lies in the compact convex hull of the connected curve
\(\Gamma([0,1])\).

We use the following connected Carathéodory lemma.  If
\(\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb R^d\) is continuous, every point of
\(\operatorname{conv}\gamma([0,1])\) is a convex combination of at
most \(d\) points of the curve.  To prove it, ordinary Carathéodory
first gives at most \(d+1\) points.  If all \(d+1\) are needed, they
are affinely independent and the target lies in the interior of their
simplex.  Order two consecutive preimages \(r_0<r_1\), and replace
the vertex \(\gamma(r_0)\) continuously by \(\gamma(r)\) as
\(r\uparrow r_1\), keeping the other \(d\) vertices fixed.  At
\(r=r_1\) two vertices coincide.  At the first parameter where the
target ceases to be an interior point of the moving simplex, either
the vertices become affinely dependent or one barycentric coordinate
is zero.  In either case the target is a convex combination of at
most \(d\) curve points.  This proves the lemma, including \(d=1\).

Applying the lemma to (18), choose \(q\leq m-1\), nodes
\(s_i\in[0,1]\), and positive weights \(\theta_i\) summing to \(1\)
such that
\[
y=\sum_{i=1}^{q}\theta_i\Gamma(s_i).
\]
Set
\[
w_i=\frac{M_0\theta_i}{\phi_0(s_i)},\qquad
\eta=\sum_{i=1}^{q}w_i\delta_{s_i}.
\tag{19}
\]
Then every coordinate is matched exactly:
\[
\int_{[0,1]}\Phi\,d\eta
=\int_{(0,1)}\Phi\,d\nu.
\]
The normalization by the positive coordinate (17), continuity on the
compact interval, and the probability measure \(\pi\) supply the
required mass control and attainment; no closure point outside
\([0,1]\) is used.

Endpoint atoms in (19) are affine data, not curvature kinks.  If
\(w_0\) is the mass at \(s=0\) and \(w_1\) the mass at \(s=1\), set
\[
\widetilde a=a+w_0,\qquad
\widetilde h=h+w_1.
\tag{20}
\]
For every evaluation at a positive point and every integral in
(10)--(11), the extended vector \(\Phi(0)\) is exactly the
contribution of the constant width \(1\), by (15)--(16).  Likewise,
\(\Phi(1)\) is exactly the contribution of the affine width \(u\).
For each remaining node \(0<s_i<1\), put
\[
t_i=s_i,\qquad d_i=\frac{w_i}{s_i}.
\tag{21}
\]
Equations (19)--(21) define \(\widetilde v\) in (6), with at most
\(m-1\) interior curvature atoms.

The moment equality following (19) now gives
\[
\mathcal A(\widetilde v)=\mathcal A(v),\qquad
\mathcal K(\widetilde v)=\mathcal K(v),
\tag{22}
\]
and preserves \(v(\rho)\).  If \(\alpha>0\), it also preserves
\(v(\alpha)\).  Thus (12) proves the equalities in item 2.

The new coefficients satisfy
\[
\widetilde a\geq a>0,\qquad
\widetilde h\geq h\geq0,\qquad d_i>0,
\]
so \(\widetilde v\) is positive, nondecreasing, and concave.  It
remains to audit the clipping alternatives.  If \(\alpha=0\), then
\(\widetilde v(0)=\widetilde a\geq a\geq\lambda\).  If
\(\alpha>0\), moment matching gives
\[
\widetilde v(\alpha)=\lambda-\alpha.
\]
Monotonicity then gives
\[
\widetilde v(0)\leq\lambda-\alpha<\lambda,
\]
and \(\widetilde v(u)+u-\lambda\) is strictly increasing, so
\(\alpha\) remains the unique left clipping point.

If \(\beta=1\), preservation of \(v(1)\) gives
\[
\widetilde v(1)=v(1)\geq1-\lambda.
\]
Concavity between
\(\widetilde g(\lambda)=\widetilde v(\lambda)>0\) and
\(\widetilde g(1)\geq0\) then keeps the right clipping endpoint at
\(1\).
If \(\beta<1\), preservation of \(v(\beta)\) gives
\[
\widetilde v(\beta)=\beta-\lambda.
\]
The function
\[
\widetilde g(u)=\widetilde v(u)-u+\lambda
\]
is concave, satisfies \(\widetilde g(\lambda)>0\), and vanishes at
\(\beta\).  Concavity makes it positive on
\((\lambda,\beta)\), while the secant-slope inequality makes it
strictly negative on \((\beta,1]\).  Hence \(\beta\) remains the
unique right clipping point.  This verifies every endpoint cell,
including equality in either endpoint condition.

Interior nodes in (21) may equal
\(\alpha,\lambda,\beta\); these are legitimate weak kink boundaries.
Nodes \(0\) and \(1\) were absorbed into the affine coefficients in
(20).  Thus the construction covers all boundary cells and proves
\(N\leq3\), with \(N\leq2\) when \(\alpha=0\).

Finally, we audit why this argument does not reduce to one curvature
atom.  In a generic interior-interior cell the four coordinates in
(13) are genuinely independent.  For example, take
\[
\alpha=\frac15,\qquad
\lambda=\frac12,\qquad
\beta=\frac45
\]
and evaluate \(\Phi\) at
\[
\frac1{10},\qquad\frac3{10},\qquad
\frac35,\qquad\frac9{10}.
\]
With rows ordered as right value, left value, \(\mathcal A\),
\(\mathcal K\), the four columns form
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1&1&8/9\\
1&2/3&1/3&2/9\\
7/5&41/20&11/5&21/10\\
1&11/12&2/5&29/90
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det=-\frac1{405}\ne0.
\tag{23}
\]
Thus neither clipping equality nor either bad functional is a linear
consequence of the other three.  A one-atom conclusion would require
a new structural inequality, not Carathéodory reduction.  The present
lemma reduces the unresolved general-width problem exactly to
\[
\widetilde v(u)
=\widetilde a+\widetilde h\,u
{}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_i\min\{u,t_i\},
\qquad N\leq3,
\]
with the atoms distributed among the four closed intervals cut by
\(\alpha,\lambda,\beta\).  Accepted one-kink cell results cover only
\(N=1\); the next finite class is the at-most-three-kink class above.
