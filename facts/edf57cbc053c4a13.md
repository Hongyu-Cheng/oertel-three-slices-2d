## Statement

Let
\[
\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3>0,\qquad
R=\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3,
\]
and define, as in accepted fact `0009896ebff12f31`,
\[
g(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)
=
\min_{S\subseteq\{1,2,3\}}
\left(
2\sum_{i\notin S}\lambda_i+
\sum_{\{i,j\}\subseteq S}\sqrt{\lambda_i\lambda_j}
\right),
\tag{1}
\]
\[
\epsilon(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)
=
\max\left\{
0,\
\min\left\{
\frac32,\
\frac{1-R}{2}+g(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)
\right\}
\right\}.
\tag{2}
\]
For \(\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}\), define the six closed faces
\[
\mathcal A_i
=
\left\{
\lambda_i\geq1+3(\lambda_j+\lambda_k)
\right\},
\tag{3}
\]
\[
\mathcal B_{ij\mid k}
=
\left\{
\left|\sqrt{\lambda_i}-\sqrt{\lambda_j}\right|
\geq\sqrt{1+3\lambda_k}
\right\}.
\tag{4}
\]
Here \(\mathcal B_{ij\mid k}=\mathcal B_{ji\mid k}\), so there are three
faces of type \(\mathcal B\).  Then the exact zero-gap locus is
\[
\boxed{
\left\{\epsilon=0\right\}
=
\bigcup_{i=1}^3\mathcal A_i
\ \cup\
\mathcal B_{12\mid3}
\ \cup\
\mathcal B_{13\mid2}
\ \cup\
\mathcal B_{23\mid1}.
}
\tag{5}
\]
All inequalities in (3)-(5) are non-strict, so every intersection and
boundary face is included.

Suppose in addition that the ratios come from the three positive caps and
positive central cell of the all-double \(Q\)-geometry.  Accepted fact
`8234d1112ec2f031` then gives the exact normalized constraints
\[
4\lambda_1\lambda_2\lambda_3\leq1+R,
\tag{6}
\]
\[
\sqrt{\lambda_1}+\sqrt{\lambda_2}+\sqrt{\lambda_3}
\leq\frac32\sqrt{1+R}.
\tag{7}
\]
Every point satisfying (5)-(7) has a unique dominant coordinate.  Relabel
it as \(\lambda_1\), while \(\lambda_2\) and \(\lambda_3\) remain
interchangeable.  Then
\[
\lambda_1>\max\{\lambda_2,\lambda_3,1\},
\qquad
\lambda_2\lambda_3<\frac12.
\tag{8}
\]
Moreover, (7) is strict on the locus (5).

Up to coordinate permutations, both face types in (5) are necessary even
after imposing (6)-(7).  The exact ratio triple
\[
\left(\frac{53}{50},\frac1{100},\frac1{100}\right)
\tag{9}
\]
lies in \(\mathcal A_1\), lies in no \(\mathcal B\)-face, and satisfies
(6)-(7) strictly.  The exact ratio triple
\[
\left(10,4,\frac1{100}\right)
\tag{10}
\]
lies in \(\mathcal B_{12\mid3}\), lies in no \(\mathcal A\)-face, and
satisfies (6)-(7) strictly.

Finally, consider any sequence of full positive-middle \(W=0\)
common-plane realizations satisfying the hypotheses of accepted facts
`0009896ebff12f31` and `c44e67089f10e853`.  Normalize each table by
\(q=q_{123}=1\), and assume its ratio triples remain in a compact subset
of \((0,\infty)^3\) while
\[
r_{123}\longrightarrow0,\qquad
\eta\longrightarrow0.
\tag{11}
\]
Every convergent ratio subsequence has a limit \(\lambda^*\) satisfying
\[
\lambda^*\in
\bigcup_i\mathcal A_i
\cup\mathcal B_{12\mid3}
\cup\mathcal B_{13\mid2}
\cup\mathcal B_{23\mid1},
\tag{12}
\]
\[
4\lambda_1^*\lambda_2^*\lambda_3^*
\leq1+\sum_i\lambda_i^*,
\qquad
\sum_i\sqrt{\lambda_i^*}
\leq\frac32\sqrt{1+\sum_i\lambda_i^*},
\tag{13}
\]
and
\[
\delta(\lambda^*)\leq
\tau\!\left(\sum_i\lambda_i^*\right),
\tag{14}
\]
where \(\delta\) is the explicit seven-term cap function and
\[
\tau(R)=
\frac{49}{
\left(\sqrt{1+R}+\sqrt{204+120R}\right)^2}
\]
from accepted fact `c44e67089f10e853`.  Thus, up to permutations, the
positive compact limit has only the two analytic face types
\(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\), together with the closed constraints
(13)-(14); condition (8) records strict consequences on each positive
limit.

## Proof

Because \(3/2>0\), equation (2) gives
\[
\epsilon=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{1-R}{2}+g\leq0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
g\leq\frac{R-1}{2}.
\tag{15}
\]
Expand the eight values in (1).  They are
\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
S&\displaystyle
2\sum_{i\notin S}\lambda_i+
\sum_{\{i,j\}\subseteq S}\sqrt{\lambda_i\lambda_j}\\ \hline
\varnothing&2R\\
\{i\}&2(\lambda_j+\lambda_k)\\
\{i,j\}&2\lambda_k+\sqrt{\lambda_i\lambda_j}\\
\{1,2,3\}&
\sqrt{\lambda_1\lambda_2}
+\sqrt{\lambda_1\lambda_3}
+\sqrt{\lambda_2\lambda_3}
\end{array}
\tag{16}
\]
with \(\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}\).  Since a minimum is at most a number
exactly when at least one entry is at most that number, (15) is the union
of the eight entrywise inequalities from (16).

For \(S=\varnothing\), the entrywise inequality is
\[
2R\leq\frac{R-1}{2},
\]
or \(3R\leq-1\), which is impossible for positive ratios.

For \(S=\{i\}\), the entrywise inequality is
\[
2(\lambda_j+\lambda_k)\leq\frac{R-1}{2}.
\]
After substituting \(R=\lambda_i+\lambda_j+\lambda_k\), this becomes
\[
\lambda_i\geq1+3(\lambda_j+\lambda_k),
\]
which is exactly \(\mathcal A_i\).

For \(S=\{i,j\}\), the entrywise inequality becomes
\[
2\lambda_k+\sqrt{\lambda_i\lambda_j}
\leq\frac{\lambda_i+\lambda_j+\lambda_k-1}{2}.
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\lambda_i+\lambda_j-2\sqrt{\lambda_i\lambda_j}
\geq1+3\lambda_k,
\]
which is exactly \(\mathcal B_{ij\mid k}\).

The last apparent region, from \(S=\{1,2,3\}\), is
\[
R-1\geq
2\left(
\sqrt{\lambda_1\lambda_2}
+\sqrt{\lambda_1\lambda_3}
+\sqrt{\lambda_2\lambda_3}
\right).
\tag{17}
\]
It is redundant.  Put \(a_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}\), and relabel so that
\[
a_1\geq a_2\geq a_3>0.
\]
Equation (17) is equivalent to
\[
(a_1-a_2-a_3)^2\geq1+4a_2a_3.
\tag{18}
\]
One must have \(a_1-a_2-a_3>0\).  Indeed, otherwise
\[
0\leq a_2+a_3-a_1\leq a_3,
\]
so the left side of (18) is at most \(a_3^2\), whereas its right side is
strictly larger than \(a_3^2\) because \(a_2\geq a_3>0\).
Consequently (18) gives
\[
a_1-a_2
\geq
a_3+\sqrt{1+4a_2a_3}.
\]
The square of the right side exceeds \(1+3a_3^2\), since its excess is
\[
2a_3\left(2a_2+\sqrt{1+4a_2a_3}-a_3\right)>0.
\]
Thus
\[
(a_1-a_2)^2\geq1+3a_3^2,
\]
which is \(\mathcal B_{12\mid3}\).  Hence the all-three region is
contained in a two-element region.  This proves the exact six-face
identity (5).

We now impose the accepted three-cap geometry.  Apply accepted fact
`8234d1112ec2f031` with central area \(q\), cap areas
\(x_i=q\lambda_i\), and total area
\[
m=q(1+R).
\]
Its product and square-root inequalities become exactly (6) and (7).
Its equality characterization says equality in (7) is possible only when
the central cell and all three caps have equal area, namely
\[
\lambda_1=\lambda_2=\lambda_3=1.
\tag{19}
\]
At (19), the four possible values in (16), by subset size, are
\[
6,\quad4,\quad3,\quad3,
\]
so \(g=3\) and
\[
\frac{1-R}{2}+g=2>0.
\]
Therefore (19) is not in (5), proving that (7) is strict throughout the
zero-gap locus.

Every \(\mathcal A_i\)-point has
\[
\lambda_i\geq1+3(\lambda_j+\lambda_k)
>\max\{1,\lambda_j,\lambda_k\}.
\tag{20}
\]
Every \(\mathcal B_{ij\mid k}\)-point has one of
\(\sqrt{\lambda_i},\sqrt{\lambda_j}\) larger than the other by at least
\(\sqrt{1+3\lambda_k}>1\).  The larger coordinate is therefore greater
than \(1\), greater than the other member of the pair, and greater than
\(\lambda_k\), because
\[
\sqrt{1+3\lambda_k}>\sqrt{3\lambda_k}>\sqrt{\lambda_k}.
\tag{21}
\]
Thus every point of (5) has a unique dominant coordinate, proving the
first part of (8).

Relabel that coordinate as \(\lambda_1\), and let
\(\lambda_2,\lambda_3\) be the two minor coordinates.  Suppose for
contradiction that
\[
\lambda_2\lambda_3\geq\frac12.
\tag{22}
\]
The product inequality (6) can be written
\[
\lambda_1(4\lambda_2\lambda_3-1)
\leq1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3.
\]
Under (22), \(4\lambda_2\lambda_3-1\geq1\), hence
\[
\lambda_1\leq1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3.
\tag{23}
\]
If the point lies in \(\mathcal A_1\), then (20) contradicts (23).  If it
lies in a \(\mathcal B\)-face, that face must contain the unique dominant
coordinate.  After possibly swapping the minor indices, it is
\(\mathcal B_{12\mid3}\), and therefore
\[
\lambda_1
\geq
\left(
\sqrt{\lambda_2}+\sqrt{1+3\lambda_3}
\right)^2
>
1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3,
\]
again contradicting (23).  This proves the strict minor-product bound in
(8).

We verify that no further elimination of either face orbit follows from
(6)-(7).  For (9),
\[
\frac{53}{50}
=1+3\left(\frac1{100}+\frac1{100}\right),
\]
so it lies on \(\mathcal A_1\).  It lies on no \(\mathcal B\)-face:
for either pair containing the first coordinate,
\[
\left(
\sqrt{\frac{53}{50}}-\frac1{10}
\right)^2
=\frac{107}{100}
-\frac15\sqrt{\frac{53}{50}}
<\frac{87}{100}
<\frac{103}{100},
\]
while the remaining pair has equal square roots.  Its product inequality
is strict because
\[
4\cdot\frac{53}{50}\cdot\frac1{100}\cdot\frac1{100}
=\frac{53}{125000}
<
\frac{52}{25}
=1+R.
\]
Also
\[
\sqrt{\frac{53}{50}}+\frac15
<
\frac{13}{10}
<
\frac{3\sqrt{13}}5
=\frac32\sqrt{\frac{52}{25}},
\]
so (7) is strict.

For (10),
\[
\left(\sqrt{10}-2\right)^2
=14-4\sqrt{10}
>
\frac65
>
\frac{103}{100}
=1+3\frac1{100},
\]
where \(\sqrt{10}<16/5\).  Hence (10) lies in
\(\mathcal B_{12\mid3}\).  It lies in no \(\mathcal A\)-face; for the
only possible dominant coordinate,
\[
10<
1+3\left(4+\frac1{100}\right)
=\frac{1303}{100}.
\]
The other two \(\mathcal A\)-inequalities fail as well, since
\[
4<1+3\left(10+\frac1{100}\right)=\frac{3103}{100},
\qquad
\frac1{100}<1+3(10+4)=43.
\]
The product inequality is strict:
\[
4\cdot10\cdot4\cdot\frac1{100}
=\frac85
<
\frac{1501}{100}
=1+R.
\]
Finally,
\[
\sqrt{10}+2+\frac1{10}
<
\frac{53}{10}
<
\frac{3\sqrt{1501}}{20}
=\frac32\sqrt{\frac{1501}{100}},
\]
where the last strict inequality follows after squaring from
\[
9\cdot1501>106^2.
\]
Thus both permutation orbits in (5) remain genuinely compatible with all
accepted three-cap constraints.

It remains to prove the compact-limit reduction.  By the strict defect
bound in accepted fact `0009896ebff12f31`, every normalized table in the
sequence satisfies
\[
0\leq\epsilon(\lambda^{(n)})<\eta_n.
\tag{24}
\]
After passing to a convergent ratio subsequence, compactness inside the
positive ratio domain gives
\[
\lambda^{(n)}\longrightarrow\lambda^*\in(0,\infty)^3.
\]
The function \(\epsilon\) is continuous because it is formed from a
finite minimum and continuous elementary functions.  Equations
(11) and (24) therefore imply
\[
\epsilon(\lambda^*)=0.
\]
The exact characterization (5) proves (12).  Constraints (6)-(7) hold
for every member of the sequence and are closed, so they pass to the
limit and give (13).

Accepted fact `c44e67089f10e853` rules out every \(W=0\) realization
with
\[
\delta(\lambda^{(n)})
\geq
\tau\!\left(R^{(n)}\right).
\]
Thus every member of the surviving sequence satisfies the strict reverse
inequality.  Both \(\delta\) and \(\tau\) are continuous on the positive
ratio domain, so passage to the limit gives (14).  Applying (8) to the
positive limit completes the finite two-orbit reduction.  Finally,
\(\eta_n\geq6r_{123}^{(n)}\), so under the normalization \(q=1\) the
second convergence in (11) already forces the first; recording both makes
the intended positive-middle boundary explicit.
