## Statement

Under the common affine-plane and exact-cell hypotheses of accepted fact
`0e51a47a9add571f`, relabel so that
\[
f_1+f_2+f_3=-1,\qquad L_1+L_2+L_3<-1,
\]
and assume \(p=|P_\varnothing|>0\), \(|Q_1|>0\), and \(|Q_2|>0\).
Set
\[
\Delta=-1-(L_1+L_2+L_3),\qquad
\mathcal T_P=\{f_i>L_i:\ i=1,2,3\},\qquad
\Theta=|\mathcal T_P|,\qquad \alpha=p/\Theta.
\]
Let \(y_1,y_2\) be the area centroids of \(Q_1,Q_2\), put
\(q_i(y)=f_i(y)+L_i\), and define
\[
A=\frac{q_1(y_2)-q_1(y_1)}{\Delta},\qquad
B=\frac{q_2(y_1)-q_2(y_2)}{\Delta},\qquad
L=\max\{A,B\},\qquad l=\min\{A,B\}.
\]
Then \(A,B>1\), and
\[
\left|
\operatorname{conv}\left(
\frac{P_\varnothing+\overline{Q_1}}2
\cup
\frac{P_\varnothing+\overline{Q_2}}2
\right)\cap R_{12}
\right|
>
\frac{pL}{\Delta l\left(1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}\right)}.
\]

## Proof

Put \(q_i=f_i+L_i\).  The constant-sum identities are
\[
\sum_{i=1}^3(f_i-L_i)=\Delta,\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^3q_i=-(\Delta+2).
\tag{1}
\]
The affine map \(x\mapsto(f_1(x),f_2(x),f_3(x))\) is an affine
isomorphism onto the plane of coordinate sum \(-1\), by accepted fact
`0e51a47a9add571f`.

We first locate the two centroids strictly.  On \(Q_1\), one has
\[
q_1\leq0,\qquad q_2>0,\qquad q_3>0.
\]
Each \(q_i\) is nonconstant, so its zero set is a line and has planar
area zero.  Since \(Q_1\) has positive area and an affine function
commutes with taking an area centroid,
\[
q_1(y_1)<0,\qquad q_2(y_1)>0,\qquad q_3(y_1)>0.
\tag{2}
\]
The same argument gives
\[
q_2(y_2)<0,\qquad q_1(y_2)>0,\qquad q_3(y_2)>0.
\tag{3}
\]
The centroids belong to \(A_2\): each is the barycenter of a bounded
measurable subset of the closed convex set \(A_2\).  Equations (2) and
(3) also show that \(y_j\in Q_j\), not merely in its closure.  From
(1),
\[
q_1(y_1)=-(\Delta+2)-q_2(y_1)-q_3(y_1)<-(\Delta+2),
\]
and similarly \(q_2(y_2)<-(\Delta+2)\).  Consequently,
\[
A>\frac{\Delta+2}{\Delta}>1,\qquad
B>\frac{\Delta+2}{\Delta}>1.
\tag{4}
\]

We next prove the simplex extrusion estimate needed below.  Let
\[
\Sigma=\{(x_1,x_2):x_1,x_2\geq0,\ x_1+x_2\leq1\},
\]
let \(E\subseteq\Sigma\) be a measurable convex set of area
\(p'\in(0,1/2]\), and let \(v=(a,-b)\), where \(a,b>0\).  Set
\(\Lambda=\max\{a,b\}\) and \(\beta=2p'\).  Then
\[
|E+[0,v]|=p'+R
\tag{5}
\]
and
\[
R\geq
\Lambda\left(1-\sqrt{1-\beta}\right)
=\frac{2\Lambda p'}{1+\sqrt{1-\beta}},
\tag{6}
\]
where \(R\) is the length of the interval
\[
\{bx_1+ax_2:(x_1,x_2)\in E\}.
\]
In particular,
\[
|E+[0,v]|
\geq
p'\left(1+\frac{2\Lambda}{1+\sqrt{1-\beta}}\right).
\tag{7}
\]

To prove this, use coordinates
\[
r=bx_1+ax_2,\qquad t=x_1/a.
\tag{8}
\]
The absolute Jacobian of \((x_1,x_2)\mapsto(r,t)\) is \(1\), and
translation by \(v\) fixes \(r\) and sends \(t\) to \(t+1\).  Every
nonempty \(r\)-fiber of \(E\) is an interval.  Extrusion by
\([0,v]\) therefore adds exactly \(1\) to the length of each such
fiber.  Fubini's theorem gives (5).

It remains to bound \(R\).  By symmetry assume \(a=\Lambda\geq b\).
If \(a>b\), the \(t\)-length of the \(r\)-section of \(\Sigma\) is
\[
\lambda(r)=
\begin{cases}
r/(ab),&0\leq r\leq b,\\[1mm]
(a-r)/(a(a-b)),&b\leq r\leq a.
\end{cases}
\tag{9}
\]
If \(a=b\), the second branch disappears and
\(\lambda(r)=r/a^2\) for \(0\leq r\leq a\).  In both cases the
decreasing rearrangement is
\[
\lambda^*(s)=\frac{1-s/a}{a},\qquad 0\leq s\leq a.
\tag{10}
\]
For \(a>b\), this follows directly from
\[
\bigl|\{r:\lambda(r)>u\}\bigr|=a(1-au),
\qquad 0<u<1/a;
\]
the same formula follows immediately from the increasing linear section
function when \(a=b\).

The \(r\)-projection of \(E\) is an interval \(I\) of length \(R\).
Its fibers lie inside the corresponding fibers of \(\Sigma\), so the
layer-cake formula and (10) give
\[
p'\leq\int_I\lambda(r)\,dr
\leq\int_0^R\lambda^*(s)\,ds
=\frac Ra-\frac{R^2}{2a^2}.
\tag{11}
\]
Since \(0\leq R/a\leq1\), solving (11) proves (6), including
\(\beta=1\), where it says \(R=a\).  The estimate is sharp.  For the
value of \(R\) in (6), take the intersection of \(\Sigma\) with the
\(r\)-slab on which \(\lambda\) takes its largest values.  That slab is
an interval in \(r\), its intersection with \(\Sigma\) is convex, its
area is \(p'\), and equality holds in (5), (6), and (7).

Return to the common affine frame.  Define
\[
\xi_i(x)=\frac{f_i(x)-L_i}{\Delta}.
\tag{12}
\]
Then \(\sum_i\xi_i=1\), and \(x\mapsto(\xi_1(x),\xi_2(x))\)
maps \(\mathcal T_P\) affinely onto the interior of \(\Sigma\).
Because \(|\Sigma|=1/2\) and \(|\mathcal T_P|=\Theta\), this map
satisfies
\[
|\xi(E_0)|=\frac{|E_0|}{2\Theta}
\tag{13}
\]
for every measurable \(E_0\subseteq\mathbb R^2\).  Thus the image
\(P=\xi(P_\varnothing)\) is convex and
\[
|P|=\frac{p}{2\Theta}=\frac{\alpha}{2}.
\tag{14}
\]
In particular \(0<\alpha\leq1\).

For a middle point \(z\), put
\[
w_i(z)=\frac{2f_i(z)}{\Delta}.
\tag{15}
\]
The linear part of \(z\mapsto(w_1(z),w_2(z))\) is twice the linear
part of \(x\mapsto(\xi_1(x),\xi_2(x))\).  Its coordinate area is
therefore four times as large.  Equation (13) becomes the exact
conversion
\[
|E_1|_{\mathrm{physical}}
=\frac{\Theta}{2}|w(E_1)|
\tag{16}
\]
for every measurable middle-plane set \(E_1\).  This proves both
Jacobian normalizations without choosing Euclidean axes adapted to the
three affine functions.

Let
\[
c_j=\left(\frac{q_1(y_j)}{\Delta},
\frac{q_2(y_j)}{\Delta}\right)
\quad(j=1,2).
\]
For \(x\in P_\varnothing\), affinity gives
\[
w_i\left(\frac{x+y_j}{2}\right)
=\xi_i(x)+\frac{q_i(y_j)}{\Delta}.
\tag{17}
\]
Hence, if
\[
V_j=\frac{P_\varnothing+y_j}{2},\qquad
\mathcal S=\operatorname{conv}(V_1\cup V_2),
\]
then
\[
w(\mathcal S)
=\operatorname{conv}\bigl((P+c_1)\cup(P+c_2)\bigr)
=P+c_1+[0,v],
\qquad v=(A,-B).
\tag{18}
\]
The last equality uses only convexity of \(P\).  Since
\(y_j\in Q_j\subseteq\overline{Q_j}\), one has
\[
\mathcal S\subseteq
\operatorname{conv}\left(
\frac{P_\varnothing+\overline{Q_1}}2
\cup
\frac{P_\varnothing+\overline{Q_2}}2
\right).
\tag{19}
\]
Also \(\mathcal S\subseteq K\): compatibility puts each \(V_j\) in
\(K\), and \(K\) is convex.

For every \(x\in P_\varnothing\), the point in \(V_1\) obtained from
\(x\) satisfies
\[
w_1<0,\qquad w_2>0,\qquad w_3>0,
\tag{20}
\]
while the corresponding point in \(V_2\) satisfies
\[
w_1>0,\qquad w_2<0,\qquad w_3>0.
\tag{21}
\]
Indeed, the positive coordinates in (20) and (21) follow directly from
\(\xi_i(x)>0\) and (2), (3).  The remaining coordinate is negative
because
\[
w_1+w_2+w_3=-\frac2\Delta.
\tag{22}
\]
The segment between these corresponding points lies in \(\mathcal S\),
and its \(w_3\)-coordinate is strictly positive throughout.

Use the following coordinates on the \(w\)-plane:
\[
r=Bw_1+Aw_2,\qquad t=w_1/A.
\tag{23}
\]
Again the absolute Jacobian is \(1\), and translation by
\(v=(A,-B)\) fixes \(r\) and increases \(t\) by \(1\).  The
\(r\)-projection \(I\) of \(P+c_1\) is an interval whose length is
exactly the number \(R\) in the extrusion lemma applied to
\(P\subseteq\Sigma\) and \(v=(A,-B)\).

Fix \(r\in I\) and choose any corresponding \(x\in P\).  Along the
translated segment in (18), \(w_1\) increases from a negative value to
a positive value, while \(w_2\) decreases from a positive value to a
negative value.  Its crossings with \(w_2=0\) and \(w_1=0\) have,
respectively,
\[
t=\frac{r}{AB},\qquad t=0.
\tag{24}
\]
Both crossings occur inside the segment, where \(w_3>0\).  At the first
crossing, (22) gives \(w_1<-2/\Delta\), and at the second it gives
\(w_2<-2/\Delta\).  Since \(r=Bw_1\) at the first crossing and
\(r=Aw_2\) at the second,
\[
r<-\frac{2B}{\Delta},\qquad
r<-\frac{2A}{\Delta}.
\tag{25}
\]
In particular \(r/(AB)<0\).  The complete interval
\[
\frac{r}{AB}\leq t\leq0
\tag{26}
\]
lies between the two crossings.  It is contained in the translated
segment and satisfies \(w_1\leq0\), \(w_2\leq0\), and \(w_3>0\).
Thus every \(r\in I\), not merely almost every one, contributes the
full interval (26) to \(w(\mathcal S\cap R_{12})\).

By (25),
\[
-\frac{r}{AB}>
\max\left\{\frac{2}{\Delta A},\frac{2}{\Delta B}\right\}
=\frac{2}{\Delta l}
\tag{27}
\]
for every \(r\in I\).  The interval \(I\) has positive length because
\(p>0\).  Integrating (26), using the unit Jacobian in (23), gives the
strict estimate
\[
|w(\mathcal S\cap R_{12})|
\geq\int_I-\frac{r}{AB}\,dr
>\frac{2R}{\Delta l}.
\tag{28}
\]
Strictness in the last inequality follows because the measurable
integrand minus \(2/(\Delta l)\) is positive at every point of the
positive-length interval \(I\).

Apply (6) with \(p'=\alpha/2\), \(\beta=\alpha\), and
\(\Lambda=L\).  Then
\[
R\geq\frac{L\alpha}{1+\sqrt{1-\alpha}}.
\tag{29}
\]
Combining (16), (28), and (29), and using \(\Theta\alpha=p\), yields
\[
|\mathcal S\cap R_{12}|
>
\frac{\Theta R}{\Delta l}
\geq
\frac{pL}{\Delta l(1+\sqrt{1-\alpha})}.
\tag{30}
\]
The requested hull contains \(\mathcal S\) by (19), so (30) proves the
claim.

When \(\alpha=1\), (11) forces \(R=L\), and every displayed expression
above remains finite; no limiting argument is needed.  Finally,
\(\overline{Q_1}\setminus Q_1\) is contained in the two lines
\(\{q_2=0\}\cup\{q_3=0\}\), and
\(\overline{Q_2}\setminus Q_2\) is contained in
\(\{q_1=0\}\cup\{q_3=0\}\).  These lines, as well as the three middle
cutting lines and the three lines \(\{f_i=L_i\}\) bounding
\(\mathcal T_P\), have planar area zero.  Moreover
\(\overline{Q_j}\subseteq A_2\) because \(A_2\) is closed.  Thus the
closures do not alter any source-cell area or the compatibility
inclusion.  The endpoints in (26) have one of \(w_1,w_2\) equal to
zero and are assigned to \(R_{12}\) by its weak inequalities, while
\(w_3\) is strictly positive.  This verifies the exact-cell convention
on every cutting line.
