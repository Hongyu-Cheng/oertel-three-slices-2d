## Statement

Let \(Q\subset\mathbb R^2\) be a two-dimensional convex polygon. For \(i=1,2,3\), let \(C_i=Q\cap H_i^+\), where \(H_i^+\) is a closed halfplane, and suppose that the interiors of \(C_1,C_2,C_3\) are pairwise disjoint. Put
\[
C_0=Q\cap H_1^-\cap H_2^-\cap H_3^-,
\qquad
A_i=|C_i|,
\qquad
A=|Q|,
\]
where \(H_i^-\) is the halfplane complementary to \(H_i^+\). Then
\[
A_0^2A\ge 4A_1A_2A_3
\]
and
\[
\sqrt{\frac{A_1}{A}}+
\sqrt{\frac{A_2}{A}}+
\sqrt{\frac{A_3}{A}}
\le \frac32.
\]
Equality in the latter inequality holds if and only if \(Q\) is a triangle and the three cutting lines are its medial lines, with \(C_i\) the three corner triangles. In particular, every zero-area or otherwise degenerate cap gives strict inequality.

## Proof

We first prove an auxiliary three-segment inequality.

Let \(K\) be a convex polygon, and let \(p_1,p_2,p_3\) occur in this cyclic order on \(\partial K\). Write
\[
W=|\operatorname{conv}\{p_1,p_2,p_3\}|.
\]
For \(i\) modulo \(3\), let \(S_i\) be the area of the part of \(K\) bounded by the boundary arc from \(p_i\) to \(p_{i+1}\) and the chord \(p_ip_{i+1}\). Then
\[
|K|=W+S_1+S_2+S_3
\]
and
\[
W^2|K|\ge 4S_1S_2S_3. \tag{1}
\]
If \(W,S_1,S_2,S_3>0\), equality in (1) forces \(K\) to be a triangle.

To prove this, first regard the \(p_i\) as vertices, subdividing edges if necessary. A standard parallel-vertex slide reduces each open boundary arc between consecutive \(p_i\)'s to one vertex while preserving \(W\) and all three \(S_i\). Explicitly, if an arc contains consecutive unmarked vertices \(u,v\), with \(a\) preceding \(u\), slide \(u\) on the line through \(u\) parallel to \(av\). The shoelace contribution involving \(u\) is unchanged because the displacement is parallel to \(v-a\). Hence the polygonal area, and therefore the area \(S_i\) of that one boundary segment, is unchanged. The admissible positions preserving convexity form an interval. At one endpoint \(u,v\), and the vertex following \(v\), become collinear, so \(v\) can be deleted. Repeating this operation gives a convex hexagon
\[
p_1,v_1,p_2,v_2,p_3,v_3
\]
with the same \(W,S_1,S_2,S_3\). Collinear limiting cases follow by continuity.

Use barycentric coordinates relative to the positively oriented triangle \(p_1p_2p_3\). Put \(r_i=S_i/W\). Convexity gives nonnegative numbers \(a_i,b_i\) such that
\[
v_1=(a_1,b_1,-r_1),\qquad
v_2=(-r_2,a_2,b_2),\qquad
v_3=(b_3,-r_3,a_3).
\]
The nonnegative coordinates express that \(v_i\) lies in the exterior cone over the corresponding side of \(p_1p_2p_3\). Convexity at \(p_1,p_2,p_3\) gives
\[
a_3b_1\ge r_3r_1,\qquad
a_1b_2\ge r_1r_2,\qquad
a_2b_3\ge r_2r_3. \tag{2}
\]
Let \(R=r_1r_2r_3\), \(X=a_1a_2a_3\), and \(Y=b_1b_2b_3\). Multiplying (2) gives \(XY\ge R^2\), and hence \(X+Y\ge2R\).

The area of the alternating triangle \(v_1v_2v_3\), divided by \(W\), is the determinant
\[
D
=
X+Y
+a_1b_2r_3+a_2b_3r_1+a_3b_1r_2-R.
\]
Each of the three mixed terms is at least \(R\) by (2), so
\[
D\ge 2R+3R-R=4R.
\]
Since \(\operatorname{conv}\{v_1,v_2,v_3\}\) lies in the convex hexagon,
\[
\frac{|K|}{W}\ge D\ge4r_1r_2r_3.
\]
This is exactly (1).

If equality holds with all four areas positive, then the alternating triangle \(v_1v_2v_3\) has the same area as the hexagon, so the hexagon itself is that triangle and each \(p_i\) lies on one of its sides. Equality is also rigid under the parallel-vertex reduction. Indeed, reverse the reductions starting from this terminal triangle. At the first reverse step, the unchanged adjacent boundary arcs supply the two supporting sides of the relevant corner triangle. Convexity confines the restored arc to that corner triangle, while preservation of its segment area forces it to fill the whole corner triangle. Thus the reverse step is trivial. Induction through the reverse sequence shows that the original \(K\) was already a triangle. This proves the auxiliary inequality and its equality statement.

We apply it to the three caps. If some \(A_i=0\), then
\[
\sum_{i=1}^3\sqrt{A_i/A}
\le \sqrt{2\sum_{i=1}^3A_i/A}
\le\sqrt2<\frac32,
\]
so suppose all three cap areas are positive.

The outer boundary portion of each cap is a connected arc of \(\partial Q\). These three open arcs are pairwise disjoint and occur cyclically. Between every two consecutive cap arcs choose a boundary point \(p_i\). Such a point belongs to all three central halfplanes and hence to \(C_0\). Index them so that the boundary arc from \(p_i\) to \(p_{i+1}\) contains the outer boundary arc of \(C_i\).

Let \(S_i\) be the corresponding boundary-segment area from the auxiliary inequality, and let
\[
W=|\operatorname{conv}\{p_1,p_2,p_3\}|.
\]
Since \(C_0\) is convex and contains the three \(p_i\),
\[
W\le A_0. \tag{3}
\]
The segment region of area \(S_i\) is convex and contains the entire outer boundary arc of \(C_i\). A polygonal halfplane cap is the convex hull of its outer boundary arc, including the two endpoints of its cutting chord. Therefore
\[
A_i\le S_i. \tag{4}
\]
Applying (1), then (3) and (4), gives
\[
A_0^2A
\ge W^2A
\ge4S_1S_2S_3
\ge4A_1A_2A_3. \tag{5}
\]
This proves the stronger product inequality. Boundary gaps reduced to points, cutting lines through vertices, and other collinear degeneracies are included by the same argument with zero-area pieces, or equivalently by taking limits along \(\partial Q\).

Set
\[
x_i=\sqrt{A_i/A},
\qquad
q=A_0/A.
\]
Since the cap interiors are disjoint,
\[
q=1-x_1^2-x_2^2-x_3^2.
\]
Dividing (5) by \(A^3\) and using \(q\ge0\) yields
\[
q\ge2x_1x_2x_3,
\]
hence
\[
x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2+2x_1x_2x_3\le1. \tag{6}
\]

We now derive the constant \(3/2\). If one \(x_i=1\), (6) forces the other two to vanish. Otherwise write \(x_1=\cos\alpha\) and \(x_2=\cos\beta\), with \(\alpha,\beta\in[0,\pi/2]\). The left side of
\[
1-x_1^2-x_2^2-x_3^2-2x_1x_2x_3
=
(1-x_1^2)(1-x_2^2)-(x_3+x_1x_2)^2
\]
is nonnegative by (6). Consequently
\[
x_3+\cos\alpha\cos\beta
\le\sin\alpha\sin\beta.
\]
Thus \(\alpha+\beta\ge\pi/2\). Put \(\gamma=\pi-\alpha-\beta\in[0,\pi/2]\). Then
\[
x_3\le-\cos(\alpha+\beta)=\cos\gamma.
\]
Since cosine is concave on \([0,\pi/2]\),
\[
x_1+x_2+x_3
\le\cos\alpha+\cos\beta+\cos\gamma
\le3\cos\frac{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}{3}
=\frac32.
\]
Equality requires equality throughout: \(\alpha=\beta=\gamma=\pi/3\) and \(x_3=\cos\gamma\). Hence
\[
x_1=x_2=x_3=\frac12,
\qquad
A_0=A_1=A_2=A_3=\frac A4. \tag{7}
\]

Assume equality. The two ends of the chain in (5) are then equal, so every inequality in that chain is equality:
\[
W=A_0,\qquad S_i=A_i,\qquad W^2A=4S_1S_2S_3.
\]
The equality case of the auxiliary three-segment inequality therefore forces \(Q\) to be a triangle. Moreover, \(C_i\subseteq R_i\), where \(R_i\) is the corresponding corner segment of \(Q\), and \(C_i\) and \(R_i\) have equal positive area. Hence they coincide, up to their zero-area boundaries.

Let the vertices of \(Q\) be \(v_1,v_2,v_3\), with \(C_i\) at \(v_i\). On the edge \(v_iv_j\), let \(\lambda_{ij}\) be the fraction from \(v_i\) to the cutting point. The same point measured from the other endpoint gives
\[
\lambda_{ij}+\lambda_{ji}=1.
\]
The corner-triangle area formula and (7) give
\[
\lambda_{ij}\lambda_{ik}=\frac14
\]
for each \(i\). Multiplying these three equations gives
\[
\prod_{\{i,j\}}\lambda_{ij}\lambda_{ji}=\frac1{64}.
\]
But every factor satisfies
\[
\lambda_{ij}\lambda_{ji}\le\frac14,
\]
with equality only when \(\lambda_{ij}=\lambda_{ji}=1/2\). Since the product of the three upper bounds is \(1/64\), equality holds on every edge. Thus all three cutting points are edge midpoints, and every cutting line is a medial line.

Conversely, the medial lines of any triangle cut off three corner triangles and leave the medial triangle, all four having area \(A/4\). The square-root sum is then \(3/2\). Zero-area caps, supporting-line caps, coincident boundary pieces, and caps equal to all of \(Q\) cannot be equality cases because the initial Cauchy bound is then strict.
